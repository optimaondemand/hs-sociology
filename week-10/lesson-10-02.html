📚 PART 1 · WHY DOES ANYONE FOLLOW THE PRESIDENT?

Max Weber asked a simple question that turns out to be profoundly difficult: why do people obey? Not merely what compels them to obey, which might be force or habit, but what kind of claim on their obedience those in authority make and what kind of belief validates that claim. Weber's answer was his famous typology of legitimate authority, first presented in Economy and Society, published posthumously in 1922. He identified three pure types: traditional, charismatic, and rational-legal.

THREE WAYS TO CLAIM THE RIGHT TO BE OBEYED
No real authority is purely any one of these. The typology is a tool for asking what basis a given authority actually rests on.
TYPE 1
Traditional
“We obey because it has always been so.”
Legitimacy from the sanctity of long-standing custom. Paradigm case: hereditary monarchy.
TYPE 2
Charismatic
“We obey because this person is extraordinary.”
Legitimacy from devotion to a leader's exceptional qualities. Unstable; does not survive the leader unchanged.
TYPE 3
Rational-Legal
“We obey because the office was filled the right way.”
Legitimacy from formally enacted rules. Attaches to the office, not the person holding it.
The typology lets us ask, of any authority, what is the basis of its claim, and what would undermine it.
KEY TERMS
Traditional Authority
Legitimacy derived from established custom, inherited status, and the sanctity of long-standing practice. The paradigm cases are hereditary monarchy and patriarchal household authority.
Charismatic Authority
From the Greek charisma, gift of grace. Legitimacy derived from the extraordinary personal qualities of an individual leader, whether heroism, prophetic power, or exceptional gift. Inherently unstable; it must be routinized to survive the charismatic leader's death.
Rational-Legal Authority
Legitimacy derived from adherence to formally enacted rules and procedures. The dominant form in modern bureaucratic states and organizations. Legitimacy attaches to the office, not the person who holds it.
Routinization of Charisma
The process by which the extraordinary authority of a charismatic leader is transformed into a stable, institutionalized form of authority, traditional or rational-legal, that can survive the leader's death.
📚 PART 2 · TRADITIONAL AUTHORITY

Traditional authority rests on the belief in the sanctity of age-old rules and the status of those exercising authority under them. The medieval king rules because kings have always ruled. His authority derives not from any particular quality he possesses but from the office he has inherited. The patriarch commands his household not because he has been elected or certified but because that is how households have always been organized. Traditional authority is inherently conservative, legitimating the present by appeal to the past. It is also personal: the traditional ruler exercises authority as a person, not merely as a function of a position.

📚 PART 3 · CHARISMATIC AUTHORITY

Charismatic authority rests on the extraordinary qualities of an individual: the prophet's revelations, the war hero's triumphs, the revolutionary's vision. It is inherently unstable because it is personal. If the charismatic leader dies, is discredited, or fails to deliver on her promises, the authority dissolves. Weber was therefore deeply interested in the routinization of charisma, the process by which the movement built around a charismatic leader is transformed into a stable institution, a church, a political party, a dynasty, that can survive without her.

Try matching each charismatic beginning to the institution its own movement typically became once the founder was gone.

⚡ MEET THE PATTERN · TAP EACH CARD
Each front names a charismatic movement at its founding. Tap to reveal the routinized institution it typically becomes, and notice what survives the transition and what does not.
A prophet's small circle of followers
A church. The prophet's personal authority becomes an office with a succession procedure. The teaching survives; the founder's unrepeatable presence does not.
A revolutionary's band of followers
A political party. The revolutionary's personal vision becomes a platform and a membership structure that can outlast any one leader, win or lose an election, and choose a successor.
A war hero's personal following
A dynasty. The hero's personal claim to command becomes a hereditary claim his descendants can hold without ever having done anything heroic themselves.
📚 PART 4 · RATIONAL-LEGAL AUTHORITY

Rational-legal authority rests on the belief in the validity of legally enacted norms and the right of those elevated to authority under such norms to issue commands. It is the dominant form of authority in modern bureaucratic states and organizations. The elected official governs not because of her ancestry or her personal charisma but because she was chosen through a legitimate process, the election. If she violates the rules of her office, she can be removed. If the process by which she was chosen was corrupted, her authority is delegitimized. The authority attaches to the office and its procedural legitimacy, not to the person who holds it.

Weber saw the rise of rational-legal authority as inseparable from the rise of bureaucracy, the mode of organization that rational-legal authority both produces and requires. That connection is the subject of next week's lessons.

📚 PART 5 · TEST IT: WHICH TYPE IS IT?

Weber's own warning is that pure types are rare. Some of the cases below genuinely combine more than one basis of legitimacy, on purpose, so judge each by what claim is actually being made rather than reaching for the first label that fits.

⚡ TRY IT · CLASSIFY THE CASE
For each case, decide which of Weber's types is the basis of the authority's legitimacy claim. One case genuinely rests on more than one type at once.
1. A city council member takes office after winning a certified election under the town's charter, and can be removed only through the process the charter specifies.
2. A newly crowned queen takes the throne because she is the eldest child of the last monarch, under centuries-old succession rules.
3. A pope is selected by a formal conclave following centuries-old procedure, and is also regarded by the faithful as specially graced for the office.
4. A small group follows a wilderness guide through a crisis because of her uncanny knack for finding water where none should be, not because of any title she holds.
5. A self-declared revolutionary leader who seized power by force claims that the people's enthusiastic support, not any law or lineage, is what justifies his continued rule.

💪 WHAT YOU NOW KNOW

Every claim to legitimate authority ultimately rests on one of three grounds: it has always been this way, this person is extraordinary, or the process that produced this office was the right one. Almost no real authority is a pure case of just one, which is not a flaw in Weber's typology but the reason it is useful. It gives you a way to ask precisely what is holding a given authority together, and what would have to fail for it to come apart. Next week takes rational-legal authority further, into the bureaucratic machinery it depends on and the costs Weber thought that machinery carries with it.

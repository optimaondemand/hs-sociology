Lesson 3.01
Tocqueville: Democracy, Equality, and Voluntary Associations
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Can a society be free, comfortable, and losing its liberty at the same time?
Tocqueville thought yes, and he thought it was democracy's characteristic danger rather than an accident. His argument runs in a chain from a condition almost everyone welcomes to an outcome almost nobody would choose. Your task today is to follow the chain and find the link you would cut.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to reconstruct Tocqueville's chain of reasoning from equality of conditions to soft despotism, in order, and name the step I find weakest.
2
I will be able to distinguish Tocqueville's technical sense of individualism from ordinary selfishness, and explain why collapsing the two ruins his argument.
3
I will be able to explain why he treats voluntary association as a school of political virtue rather than a convenient way of getting things done.
📚 PART 1 · THE YOUNG FRENCHMAN IN AMERICA
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
A French aristocrat arrives in America in 1831 to study its prisons. Predict: what does he end up writing eight hundred pages about? (No wrong answer.)

In May 1831 a twenty-five-year-old French magistrate named Alexis de Tocqueville arrived in New York Harbor with his friend Gustave de Beaumont. They had official permission from the French government to study the American prison system, then considered a model of modern penology. Tocqueville had a larger purpose. He had come to examine the first great democratic republic in history, to find out what equality actually looked like once it was organised into institutions, and to ask whether the democratic future toward which Europe seemed to be moving should be welcomed or feared.

What he found astonished him. Americans formed associations for everything. To build a road they formed an association. To found a school they formed an association. To reform a social evil they formed an association. This habit was not incidental to American democracy. Tocqueville came to see it as democracy's lifeblood, and as precisely the thing France, having destroyed its old intermediary institutions in the Revolution, most dangerously lacked.

WHERE THE CHAIN CAN BE BROKEN
Voluntary associations. They cut the link between step 2 and step 3 by rebuilding horizontal bonds where the vertical ones used to be. This is why Tocqueville treats them as a remedy rather than a pleasant feature of American life.

Tocqueville's fundamental insight is that democracy is not primarily a set of political institutions but a social condition. It is the equalisation of ranks, the dissolution of hereditary hierarchy, the emergence of a society in which no person stands naturally above or below any other. This condition has been spreading through Western history for seven centuries, and in his view it is irresistible. The question is not whether equality will arrive but what kind of equality it brings with it.

The danger he identifies is one that equality itself generates. In a hierarchical society each person knows his place inside a network of vertical obligations, owed upward to a lord and downward to a vassal, sideways to a guild and outward to a church. Those obligations were frequently oppressive. They were also genuinely social, because each of them connected a person to a community larger than his own household. When hierarchy dissolves, the vertical bonds dissolve with it.

What replaces them? If nothing does, then each person faces the world alone, attached only to the small circle of people he personally knows and loves. That is what Tocqueville means by individualism, and the word is doing technical work here that its ordinary use does not.

📚 PART 3 · THE WORD THAT GETS MISREAD

Tocqueville coined individualism for a condition he thought had no name yet. Modern readers almost always hear the wrong thing, and the mistake is not harmless: it makes his argument look like ordinary moralising about selfish people, which is not what it is.

Ordinary selfishnessTocqueville's individualism
What it isA vice. Loving yourself more than others.A disposition. Withdrawing from public life into private life.
Who has itSome people, in every society, always.Anyone, and increasingly everyone, under conditions of equality.
Its originA defect of character.A structural effect of a social condition.
Its remedyMoral correction of the person.Institutions that give people something public to do.
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Reading individualism as selfishness turns Tocqueville into a scold and makes his remedy incoherent. If the problem were bad character, voluntary associations would be an odd cure. They work, on his account, precisely because the problem is not a defect in people. It is an absence of anything public for decent people to do.
⚡ TRY IT · WHICH IS IT?
For each case, decide what Tocqueville would call it. Judge by his definition, not by whether you approve of the person.
1. A woman devotes herself entirely to her family and her closest friends, gives generously to them, and has no involvement in anything beyond that circle.
2. A central administration efficiently manages roads, schools, and welfare so well that citizens no longer meet to decide anything about them.
3. A man cheats his business partners to enrich himself.
4. A woman spends her Saturdays on the committee of a local volunteer fire company alongside neighbours she barely knows.
5. A man votes in every election, follows the news closely, and belongs to nothing and attends nothing.
📚 PART 4 · ASSOCIATIONS AS SCHOOLS

Tocqueville's solution is characteristically American: civic association. A person who takes part in a voluntary association learns through experience that his interests are bound up with other people's. He practises deliberation, compromise, and collective action. He acquires a stake in public life. He picks up the habits of a citizen rather than the habits of a subject.

He is explicit that associations are not merely instrumental. They are not just efficient ways of getting things done. He calls them schools, and he means it. The person who has organised a mutual aid society, served on a local school board, or helped run a volunteer fire company has exercised the exact capacities that democratic self-government requires. She has learned to govern herself in relation to others, which is the foundational civic skill.

A society of people with those habits can resist the drift toward soft despotism. A society of people who have only their private lives cannot, because when the immense tutelary power offers to take the trouble of governing off their hands, they will have no reason and no practice to refuse.

▶  Decide in your notebook which link in the chain you would cut, and write one sentence defending it. Then open this.
There is no single right answer, but the links are not equally strong. Step 1 is close to a historical observation and hard to dispute. Step 4 is a prediction, and predictions can be tested against the two centuries since. The most contested link is step 2 to step 3: does the loss of hierarchical bonds actually produce withdrawal, or could new horizontal bonds have formed on their own without anyone designing them? If you think they form on their own, Tocqueville's alarm looks overdone and his remedy unnecessary. If you think they do not, then the health of associations becomes a genuine measure of a democracy's condition, and their decline becomes a real warning. That question is still live, and you will meet it again in Week 8.
📍 CASE IN POINT · THE NEW ENGLAND TOWNSHIP, 1830

The smallest unit Tocqueville studied was the New England township. “Its average population is from two to three thousand,” he reports, adding in a note that “In 1830 there were 305 townships in the State of Massachusetts, and 610,014 inhabitants.” Day-to-day business belonged to a few elected officers, the selectmen, but new undertakings went back to the whole body: “If, for instance, a school is to be established, the selectmen convoke the whole body of the electors on a certain day at an appointed place.” And the body could summon itself: “if ten citizens are desirous of submitting a new project to the assent of the township, they may demand a general convocation of the inhabitants.”

This is the concrete case behind his claim that associations are schools. The township taught self-government by handing ordinary residents real decisions about roads, schools, and rates. He drew the figures from Massachusetts statutes and a town-officer’s manual, which he cites; the judgment about what the numbers mean is his own.

Source: Tocqueville, Democracy in America I.5, “Limits of the Township” and “Authorities of the Township in New England” (1835), trans. Henry Reeve (public domain).
📚 PART 5 · WHY HE KEEPS COMING BACK

Tocqueville's account of association anticipates several thinkers this course examines in detail. Durkheim's account of the intermediate institutions that hold anomie at bay, Nisbet's argument for the necessity of community, and Putnam's analysis of social capital are all, in a sense, working out the implications of the same diagnosis. The question of what stands between the individual and the centralised state, and what happens when that middle ground is hollowed out, is one of the permanent questions of modern sociology. You will see it again in Weeks 7, 8, and 17, and it is the question the Community Flourishing Capstone finally asks you to answer for a real place.

💪 WHAT YOU NOW KNOW

You have followed an argument in which nobody behaves badly and the outcome is still bad. That shape is worth noticing, because it is what distinguishes a sociological explanation from a moral one. Tocqueville is not saying democratic citizens are worse people than aristocratic subjects were. He is saying that a change in social structure changes what decent people find themselves doing, and that the change is invisible to the people it is happening to. On Wednesday Durkheim gives that idea a name and tries to make a science out of it.

PRIMARY SOURCE 3B
What Is a Social Fact?
Durkheim, The Rules of Sociological Method, Chapter I.
💡 WHERE THIS FITS
This is the chapter Wednesday's lesson was built from. The definition you met there appears here at the very end, in the same words, and that placement is deliberate on Durkheim's part. He does not open with it. He earns it across ten pages and then states it. Reading the chapter is largely a matter of watching a definition get assembled.
📖 WHY YOU ARE READING THIS

This is the foundational text of Durkheim's sociology. You already have the lesson's account of social facts. Now you get his own words, and the thing to attend to is how he argues rather than what he concludes. He is trying to establish a new science, and a new science needs an object of study that belongs to it alone.

The chapter moves in seven steps. Track them as you read and you will find the argument much easier to hold.

HOW THE CHAPTER IS BUILT
1
Clear the ground. If everything in society counts as social, sociology has no subject of its own and dissolves into biology and psychology.
2
Point at examples. Duties as brother, husband, citizen. Beliefs the believer finds already there. Language, money, professional practice.
3
Name the first mark. These exist outside individual consciousness. They were there before you and did not wait for you.
4
Name the second mark. They constrain, and the proof is what happens when you resist. Law reacts. Opinion reacts. Ridicule reacts. Even indirect constraint ruins the industrialist who ignores it.
5
Answer the obvious objection. Does constraint insult human freedom? He says no, and briskly.
6
Widen the category. Social currents in a crowd have no fixed organisation and are social facts all the same. This is the hardest part of the chapter.
7
Confirm from a familiar case, then define. Education is constraint made visible, and only then does the definition arrive.
🎯 READ FOR THESE FOUR QUESTIONS
  1. Durkheim defines social facts as ways of acting, thinking, and feeling external to the individual. Why does he include feeling? Can a feeling really be external to the person who feels it?
  2. He argues that social facts can be recognised by the resistance they offer when you try to violate them. Give an example he does not use, where you have felt that resistance yourself.
  3. He wants sociology to have an object of study irreducibly its own, not reducible to psychology or biology. Why does that matter for establishing sociology as a legitimate science? What would be lost if social facts could be explained entirely by psychology?
  4. Near the end he addresses the objection that he is materialising social life by treating it as a thing. How does he answer, and do you find the answer convincing?
💡 ABOUT THIS TRANSLATION
Durkheim wrote in French. This English rendering was prepared for this course from the 1895 original. Two words are worth flagging before you meet them. Contrainte is translated as constraint, and it covers everything from a law to a raised eyebrow, so do not read it as force. Conscience in French means both consciousness and conscience, which is why the phrase individual consciousness below carries more moral weight in the original than it does in English.
THE PROBLEM WITH THE WORD "SOCIAL"

The question is all the more necessary because the term is commonly used with little precision. It is ordinarily applied to almost every phenomenon that occurs within society, provided it exhibits some degree of generality and possesses some social interest. But if this were so, there would be, so to speak, scarcely any human event that could not be called social. Every individual drinks, sleeps, eats, and reasons, and society has every interest in seeing that these functions are carried out regularly. If, therefore, such facts were social facts, sociology would have no object uniquely its own, and its field would merge with those of biology and psychology.

A DISTINCT CLASS OF PHENOMENA

In reality, however, every society contains a distinct class of phenomena that differ in clearly defined ways from those studied by the other natural sciences. When I fulfill my duties as a brother, a husband, or a citizen, when I carry out the obligations I have undertaken, I am discharging duties that are defined outside myself and independently of my own actions, in law and in custom. Even when these duties accord with my personal feelings and I inwardly acknowledge their reality, they nonetheless remain objective; for I did not create them, but received them through education. Indeed, how often do we remain ignorant of the full extent of our obligations and have to consult the law and its authorized interpreters in order to discover them?

Likewise, the believer finds the beliefs and practices of his religious life already established at the moment of birth. If they existed before him, they necessarily exist outside him. The system of signs I use to express my thoughts, the monetary system by which I pay my debts, the instruments of credit I employ in my commercial dealings, the practices followed in my profession, and so forth, all function independently of the particular use I make of them. Take every member of society one by one, and the same observation may be made of each. We are thus confronted with ways of acting, thinking, and feeling that possess the remarkable characteristic of existing independently of individual consciousness.

CONSTRAINT, AND HOW TO DETECT IT

Not only are these modes of conduct and thought external to the individual; they are also endowed with an imperative and coercive power by virtue of which they impose themselves upon him, whether he wills it or not. To be sure, when I conform to them willingly, this coercion is scarcely felt, if it is felt at all, because it is then unnecessary. Yet coercion remains an intrinsic characteristic of these facts, and this is evident from the way it manifests itself as soon as I attempt to resist.

If I try to violate legal rules, they react against me, either by preventing my action if there is still time, by annulling it and restoring matters to their proper condition if the act has already been committed and can be remedied, or by compelling me to pay the penalty if no other remedy is possible. Is it merely a question of moral precepts? Public opinion restrains every act that offends them through the vigilance it exercises over the conduct of citizens and through the specific sanctions at its disposal.

In other cases the constraint is less forceful, but it nonetheless exists. If I refuse to conform to the conventions of society, if, for example, I dress without regard for the customs observed in my country and social class, the ridicule I provoke and the social distance others place between themselves and me produce effects that, though milder, are analogous to formal punishment.

Elsewhere the constraint, though indirect, is no less effective. I am not legally required to speak French with my fellow citizens or to use the official currency, yet it is practically impossible for me to do otherwise. If I attempted to escape this necessity, my effort would fail miserably. As an industrialist, nothing forbids me from employing the methods and techniques of a previous century; yet if I were to do so, I would almost certainly ruin myself. Even when, in practice, I succeed in freeing myself from these rules and violating them successfully, I can never do so without first struggling against them. And even if they are ultimately overcome, they sufficiently demonstrate their constraining power by the resistance they oppose. No innovator, however successful, has ever pursued his enterprise without encountering opposition of this kind.

WHY THEY ARE NEITHER BIOLOGICAL NOR PSYCHOLOGICAL

We are therefore confronted with a class of facts possessing highly distinctive characteristics. They consist of ways of acting, thinking, and feeling that exist outside the individual and are endowed with a coercive power by virtue of which they impose themselves upon him. Consequently, they cannot be confused with organic phenomena, since they consist of representations and actions; nor can they be identified with psychological phenomena, which exist only in and through the individual consciousness. They therefore constitute a new category of phenomena, and it is to them that the designation social ought properly to be given and reserved.

The term is appropriate to them because, since they do not have the individual as their underlying basis, they can have no other substrate than society itself, whether society considered as a whole, or one of the partial groups it contains, such as religious communities, political schools, literary circles, professional associations, and the like. Moreover, the designation belongs to them alone, for the word social has a definite meaning only if it is restricted to phenomena that fall into none of the categories of facts already recognized and named. They therefore constitute the proper subject matter of sociology.

THE OBJECTION ABOUT FREEDOM

It is true that the word constraint, by which we define these facts, may alarm ardent advocates of absolute individualism. Because they maintain that the individual is perfectly autonomous, they imagine that he is diminished whenever it is suggested that he does not depend solely upon himself. Yet since it is now beyond dispute that most of our ideas and inclinations are not fashioned by us but come to us from outside, they can enter into us only by imposing themselves upon us. That, and nothing more, is what our definition means.

A footnote is omitted here, in which Durkheim observes that social constraint is not necessarily incompatible with individual personality.
SOCIAL CURRENTS

The examples we have thus far cited, legal rules, moral rules, religious dogmas, financial systems, and the like, are all constituted beliefs and established practices. From this one might conclude that a social fact exists only where there is a definite organization. Yet there are other facts which, although they lack these crystallized forms, possess the same objectivity and the same influence over the individual. These are what may be called social currents.

Thus, in an assembly, the great waves of enthusiasm, indignation, or pity that arise have no individual consciousness as their point of origin. They come to each of us from without and are capable of carrying us along despite ourselves. Certainly, if I surrender myself to them completely, I may not consciously feel the pressure they exert. But that pressure becomes unmistakable the moment I try to resist it. Let an individual attempt to oppose one of these collective manifestations, and the very sentiments he rejects turn against him.

If this external coercive force reveals itself so clearly in cases of resistance, it follows that it exists, though unconsciously, in the opposite cases as well. We are then victims of an illusion that leads us to believe we have ourselves produced what has in fact been imposed upon us from without. The ease with which we yield to these influences conceals the pressure we undergo, but it does not eliminate it. Air remains heavy, though we no longer feel its weight.

Even when we have contributed willingly to a common emotional experience, the impression we receive is altogether different from what we would have felt had we been alone. Thus, once the assembly has dispersed, once these social influences have ceased to act upon us and we are once again alone with ourselves, the emotions through which we have passed strike us as something foreign, something in which we no longer recognize ourselves. We then realize that we experienced them far more than we created them. Indeed, they may even fill us with horror because they were so contrary to our own nature.

In this way, individuals who are ordinarily quite harmless may, when gathered into a crowd, be swept into acts of atrocity. What we have said of these temporary outbursts applies equally to the more enduring currents of opinion that constantly arise around us, whether throughout society as a whole or within more limited circles, concerning religious, political, literary, artistic, and similar matters.

EDUCATION AS THE PROOF

This definition of the social fact may also be confirmed by a familiar observation: one need only examine how children are raised. Looking at the facts as they are, and as they have always been, it is immediately evident that every education consists in a continuous effort to impose upon the child ways of seeing, feeling, and acting that he would never have acquired spontaneously.

From the earliest days of life, we compel the child to eat, drink, and sleep at regular hours. We compel him to be clean, to remain quiet, and to obey. Later we compel him to take account of others, to respect customs and conventions, to work, and so forth. If, in time, this constraint ceases to be felt, it is because it gradually gives rise to habits and internal dispositions that render it unnecessary. Yet these dispositions replace constraint only because they themselves are its product.

Durkheim's discussion of collective morphology and social structure is omitted here.
THE DEFINITION

Our definition will therefore encompass the entire class of phenomena if we say:

A social fact is every way of acting, whether fixed or not, that is capable of exercising an external constraint upon the individual; or, again, every way of acting that is general throughout a given society while at the same time possessing an existence of its own, independent of its individual manifestations.

✏️ AFTER YOU READ

Write short answers to the four questions in your Sociological Journal document. Question 2 will feed directly into tonight's journal entry, so answer that one with a real example.

▶  Answer question 1 in your notebook first, then open this.
Including feeling is the boldest word in the definition, and Durkheim knows it. His defence is the passage on social currents. Waves of enthusiasm, indignation, or pity in a crowd are undeniably felt by individuals, yet he argues they did not originate in any individual, they arrive from outside, and they can carry a person somewhere they would never have gone alone. His evidence is the aftermath: once the assembly disperses, the emotion strikes you as foreign, as something you underwent rather than produced. Whether that settles it is genuinely arguable. A critic could say the feelings were always yours and the crowd merely triggered them, which would put the whole phenomenon back inside psychology. Durkheim's reply would be that a trigger cannot explain why the pattern is so regular across whole populations. That is the same move he makes with suicide rates, and it is the move his entire discipline rests on.
▶  Decide in your notebook whether his answer to the freedom objection is good enough. Then open this.
Notice how quickly he handles it, and ask whether the speed is confidence or evasion. His answer is essentially deflationary: constraint means only that our ideas come to us from outside and must therefore impose themselves, and nothing more sinister is intended. That is a fair clarification of his usage. It does not fully meet the objection, because the worry was never about the word. The worry is that if what feels most like your own judgment was installed from outside, it is unclear what remains of you to do the judging. Durkheim does not answer that here, and arguably never answers it. You may decide that a science of society does not owe an answer to a question in philosophy of mind. You may also decide that a discipline claiming your convictions are collective in origin owes some account of what is left over. Both positions are defensible. Neither is the lazy one.

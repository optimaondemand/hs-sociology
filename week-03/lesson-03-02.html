📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
If a force acts on everyone and nobody can see it, how would you prove it exists?
This is the lesson the whole discipline rests on. Durkheim needed to show that there are real things which are not in anyone's head and not in anyone's body, and he chose the least promising possible test case to show it.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to apply Durkheim's three criteria to a behaviour that feels chosen, and reach a defensible verdict.
2
I will be able to explain why Durkheim chose suicide as his demonstration case, and what would have counted as failure.
3
I will be able to say why social facts are hard to see from inside, and what intellectual effort seeing them requires.
📚 PART 1 · THE MAN WHO COUNTED SUICIDES

In the 1890s a French professor named Emile Durkheim did something that seemed absurd on its face. He sat down with government mortality records and tried to discover the sociology of suicide.

Suicide looks like the most private of all acts. It is the most interior, the most individual, the furthest removed from anything social. If any human behaviour could be explained purely by the psychology of the person who performed it, this seems to be the one. That is exactly why Durkheim chose it. A demonstration that works on the hardest case works everywhere.

What he found in the records disturbed the assumption entirely. The rates were not random. They held steady year over year within the same country, and they varied systematically across countries, religions, and social conditions. Protestant nations showed higher rates than Catholic ones. Unmarried people died by suicide more often than married people. Soldiers more than civilians in some circumstances, civilians more than soldiers in others. When social conditions changed, the rates changed with them, predictably.

Whatever was driving those numbers was not individual psychology alone. Something larger was at work, something that outlasted any particular person while acting on all of them. Durkheim called it a social fact, and sociology properly begins with the recognition of it. He published the full study as Suicide in 1897, two years after laying out the method in The Rules of Sociological Method.

💡 WHAT THE ARGUMENT DOES NOT CLAIM
Durkheim is not saying that society causes any particular person's death, or that individual circumstances do not matter. His claim is about rates, not about individuals. A rate is a property of a population and cannot be a property of a person. That distinction is the whole move, and readers who miss it think he is saying something both obvious and cruel, when he is saying something neither.
📚 PART 2 · DURKHEIM IN CONTEXT

Emile Durkheim was born in 1858 in Epinal, in eastern France, the son of a rabbi. He was expected to follow his father into religious life and turned instead toward philosophy, and eventually toward the new discipline he would spend his career founding. He studied in Germany, where he encountered the early ambitions of the social sciences, and returned to France to teach at Bordeaux and later at the Sorbonne.

The France he inhabited was in the middle of a crisis of coherence. The Revolution of 1789 had overturned the old religious and monarchical order. Industrialisation was drawing millions of people off the land and into cities. The old bonds of parish, guild, village, and estate were dissolving faster than new ones could form. Durkheim watched his society struggle to hold itself together and asked, as a scientist rather than a partisan, what holds societies together at all and what happens when those bonds fray. His answer begins with the social fact. Notice that this is the same disruption you met in Week 1 and the same anxiety you met in Tocqueville on Monday. Three thinkers, one problem.

📚 PART 3 · THE DEFINITION, TAKEN APART

In the first chapter of The Rules of Sociological Method (1895), Durkheim offers a definition worth reading slowly. You will read the full chapter on Thursday.

“A social fact is every way of acting, whether fixed or not, that is capable of exercising an external constraint upon the individual; or, again, every way of acting that is general throughout a given society while at the same time possessing an existence of its own, independent of its individual manifestations.”

Durkheim, The Rules of Sociological Method, ch. I

Three conditions are packed into that sentence, and a candidate has to satisfy all three.

THE THREE-GATE TEST
A behaviour is a social fact only if it passes all three. One failure and it is not one.
CANDIDATE
Any way of acting, thinking, or feeling
→
GATE 1
External
Did it exist before me, and will it outlast me?
It comes from outside. Not invented by you, not chosen, not derived from your psychology or biology.
→
GATE 2
General
Does it hold across the group, not just for me?
It characterises a collective. This generality is part of what makes it social in the first place.
→
GATE 3
Constraining
Does it push back when I resist it?
Not necessarily by force. Often by nothing more than the quiet pressure of shared expectation, which turns out to be enough.
Passes all three  →  social fact
The third gate is the one to test with. Constraint is invisible until you push against it, which is why Durkheim keeps describing what happens when someone tries.

External. A social fact reaches the individual from outside. The French language existed before any living speaker of French was born and will exist after they die. Nobody invented it and everybody inherits it. The same holds for legal systems, religious practices, economic institutions, table manners, and the academic calendar.

General. A social fact is widespread. It characterises a group rather than a person. Everyone in a given society speaks the dominant language, participates in the legal system, observes certain holidays, uses money, and attends school. That generality is part of what makes it social, because it belongs to the collective rather than to anyone in it.

Constraining. Social facts press back when you resist them. Speak ungrammatically enough and people will fail to understand you, or will judge you for it. Break the law and the state responds. Ignore the conventions of polite behaviour at dinner and you will feel, and cause others to feel, a real social discomfort. The constraint is rarely violent and often invisible. Much of the time it works through nothing but the quiet pressure of shared expectation. Note what this means for someone who violates a norm. They do not merely risk a punishment. They encounter the weight of an entire social order arranged against the act.

📍 CASE IN POINT · DURKHEIM’S OWN COUNTRYMEN AS THE TEST CASE

Durkheim’s first examples of a social fact are not drawn from a distant society but from the France he lived in. “I am not legally required to speak French with my fellow citizens or to use the official currency, yet it is practically impossible for me to do otherwise. If I attempted to escape this necessity, my effort would fail miserably.” He extends the point to work: “As an industrialist, nothing forbids me from employing the methods and techniques of a previous century; yet if I were to do so, I would almost certainly ruin myself.”

No statute compels any of this. The constraint is real, external to any one person, and enforced by consequences rather than police. That combination is what the definition in this lesson is built to capture. Test it: which of the three cases (language, currency, method) would survive if every individual Frenchman privately preferred otherwise?

📚 PART 4 · SIX WORTH EXAMINING
Language
You did not choose its vocabulary, grammar, or rules of politeness, yet it structures nearly every thought you express. Try holding a complex idea without language and you will discover how thoroughly it has formed you. Moving between registers, speaking one way in class and another with friends and another in an interview, means navigating a structure you did not build.
Money
A twenty-dollar bill has no intrinsic value. It is paper and ink. It functions as money only because a whole society agrees, tacitly and continuously, to treat it as such. That agreement is nobody's decision. Withdraw the collective confidence and the bill becomes worthless overnight, which has happened more than once.
Law
Laws constrain before any violation occurs. You do not own your neighbour's car. The law does not have to act on you at this moment in order to be real. It structures what is possible for you, constantly and invisibly.
Manners
Nobody taught you to feel embarrassed when someone burps at a formal dinner, and yet you reliably do. The feeling belongs to you. The standard producing it does not. It was there before you arrived and will persist after you leave.
Holidays
You did not decide that Thanksgiving falls in November, that Christmas involves family, or that the Fourth of July involves fireworks. These were handed to you. They organise the year, create shared experience, and bind communities across time, and no individual invented any of it.
School
Compulsory education did not arise from the wishes of any child or parent. It emerged from economic needs, political decisions, and cultural assumptions about childhood, and it now shapes the daily life of every young person in the society.

📚 PART 6 · WHY THEY ARE SO HARD TO SEE

Here is the paradox Durkheim insists on. Social facts are among the most powerful forces acting on human beings and among the hardest to notice. The reason is that they are internalised. Language does not feel external, because you spoke it before you had words to describe it. Norms of politeness do not feel like constraints, because you have followed them so long that they now feel like your own preferences. What begins outside becomes, through the long work of socialisation, part of what feels most naturally and most privately you.

This is precisely why sociology takes deliberate intellectual effort. To study social facts scientifically, Durkheim argues, one has to learn to treat them as things, as external objects of study, rather than as the obvious background conditions of life. The sociologist has to make the familiar strange, examining language, money, law, and custom the way a naturalist examines a specimen, asking what it is, how it works, and what it produces. You practised exactly this move in Week 2 with Miner's Nacirema. Durkheim is telling you it is not a party trick but the method.

Plato made a related point in a different idiom. The prisoners in the allegory of the cave (Republic VII) mistake shadows for reality because shadows are all they have ever seen. They do not know they are in a cave. The philosopher's task is to turn around, look at what casts the shadows, and report back, and Durkheim would say the sociologist's task is the same. The turning is painful and disorienting in both accounts. In both, it is where understanding starts.

▶  In your notebook, write down what evidence would have shown Durkheim was wrong about suicide. Then open this.
If rates had fluctuated randomly year to year, or had shown no systematic variation across countries, religions, and marital conditions, or had failed to shift when social conditions shifted, then there would have been nothing to explain above the level of individual psychology, and his case would have collapsed. This matters more than the finding does. A claim that no possible evidence could contradict is not a scientific claim, and Durkheim's whole project was to establish that sociology could make claims of the kind that evidence can bear on. When you meet a sociological argument in this course, that is the question worth asking of it: what would have counted as failure? Several arguments you meet later will not answer it well, and noticing that is part of the work.

💪 WHAT YOU NOW KNOW

Social facts are real. They do not reduce to the psychology of individuals and cannot be explained by biology. They exist at the level of society, external and general and constraining, and they shape behaviour in ways individuals rarely notice, because the shaping is finished by the time anyone is capable of reflecting on it. This is the foundational claim of sociology as Durkheim conceived it. Before you can study institutions, inequality, religion, or political life sociologically, you have to accept that a society is not a collection of persons who happen to be standing near one another. It is a structure with its own reality, its own forces, and its own laws of motion.

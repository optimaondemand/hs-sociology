📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Why does every known society regulate marriage, when the rules vary so widely?
Marriage feels like the most personal decision a person makes. It is also, in every society ever studied, one of the most heavily regulated. Holding both of those facts at once, rather than picking the comfortable one, is the whole task of this lesson.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to reconstruct the functions anthropologists attribute to marriage as a social institution, and explain why each one is social rather than merely personal.
2
I will be able to distinguish patrilineal from matrilineal descent and endogamy from exogamy, and distinguish what a society's marriage rules permit from what most of its members practice.
3
I will be able to evaluate Tocqueville's claim that democratic equality changes marriage from a formal bond into an emotional one, and identify the cost he attaches to that gain.
📚 PART 1 · WHO COUNTS AS FAMILY?
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Every known human society has some institution recognized as marriage, however different the specific rules. Before reading on: what is your best guess for why?

In 1831, when Tocqueville arrived in America, he was struck by the quality of American marriages. Compared to the arranged marriages of the French aristocracy, American marriages were freely chosen and, in his observation, genuinely affectionate. He attributed this partly to the condition of equality. In a society where no one held hereditary rank, there was less reason to marry for status and more freedom to marry for affection. He also noted, however, that the very freedom of American marriage placed an unusual moral burden on women, who were expected to be simultaneously free choosers and guardians of domestic virtue in a way that older, more structured arrangements had not required.

Tocqueville's observation points to a basic sociological truth. The form that marriage takes in any society reflects and reinforces that society's broader values and structures. Marriage is never merely a private arrangement between two individuals; it is a social institution that manages property, establishes kinship, determines inheritance, and organizes the care of children in ways that affect the entire community. To understand a society's marriage practices is to understand something fundamental about how it organizes power, love, and obligation. You will read Tocqueville's own account of this shift on Tuesday, in Democracy in America II.3, Chapters VIII to X.

📚 PART 2 · KEY TERMS
Marriage
A socially recognized and legally or ritually sanctioned union between persons that establishes rights and obligations regarding sexual access, reproduction, domestic labor, property, and the parentage of children. Every known society has some form of marriage institution.
Monogamy
From the Greek monos (one) and gamos (marriage). Marriage between one man and one woman at a time. Required by law in most modern states, and the most common form of marriage in practice even in most societies whose rules permit other forms.
Polygamy
From the Greek polys (many) and gamos. Marriage to more than one spouse at a time. Polygyny (one husband, multiple wives) is permitted by the rules of most societies in the anthropological record, though practiced by a minority of men even where it is allowed (Part 4). Polyandry (one wife, multiple husbands) is rare but documented, and often involves brothers marrying the same wife.
Endogamy / Exogamy
Endogamy requires marriage within a defined group (clan, caste, religion, ethnicity); exogamy requires marriage outside the group. Both serve to regulate social boundaries and manage alliances between groups.
Patrilineal / Matrilineal
Systems of descent and inheritance traced through the father's line (patrilineal) or the mother's line (matrilineal). Which line matters determines who is kin to whom and what is inherited by whom.
📚 PART 3 · THE SOCIAL FUNCTIONS OF MARRIAGE
WHAT MARRIAGE REGULATES
Four functions anthropologists find recurring across otherwise very different marriage systems.
FUNCTION 1
Sexual access
Specifies who may have sexual relations with whom, preventing the competition that would otherwise threaten order.
FUNCTION 2
Parentage
Establishes legally and socially recognized parentage, wherever inheritance, succession, or identity depend on knowing one's parents.
FUNCTION 3
Resources and labor
Pools property and divides work between spouses, in a pattern that reflects and reinforces broader social structure.
FUNCTION 4
Emotional bond
Provides companionship and affection, weighted very differently across periods, and most heavily in the modern West.
The first three functions appear almost everywhere. The fourth is real, but its priority is historically recent, which is worth remembering before Tuesday's reading.

Anthropologists identify several recurring functions that marriage institutions perform. The regulation of sexual access is the most basic: by specifying who may have sexual relations with whom, marriage prevents the sexual competition that would otherwise threaten social order. The determination of parentage is closely related: in societies where inheritance, succession, and social identity depend on knowing one's father and mother, marriage provides the institutional framework for establishing legally recognized parentage. The pooling of resources and labor is a third function: married households are, in most societies, economic as well as affective units, and the division of labor between spouses reflects and reinforces broader social structures.

The emotional and companionate functions of marriage have been differently weighted at different periods. In pre-modern Europe, marriage among the nobility was primarily a political and economic arrangement between families; affection might follow, or might not, but it was not the primary purpose. Among the urban middle classes of the nineteenth century, the Romantic conception of marriage as a union of souls became increasingly influential, eventually producing the modern Western ideal of companionate marriage in which emotional compatibility is the primary criterion for partner selection.

📚 PART 4 · WHICH FORM IS MOST COMMON?

Ask which form of marriage has been most common across human history and a quick answer offers itself: polygyny. The anthropological record supports that answer up to a point. George Murdock's Ethnographic Atlas, a coded record of more than 1,200 societies (Murdock et al., World Cultures 10, 1999), is the standard reference. A widely cited tally of it counts 186 societies as monogamous, 453 as permitting occasional polygyny, 588 as practicing polygyny more frequently, and 4 as polyandrous. Roughly 85 percent of the societies on record allow a man more than one wife.

That tally answers a narrower question than it first appears to. It records what each society's rules permit, not what most of its members do. A rule allowing several wives does not supply them. Men and women are born in roughly equal numbers, so every man with two wives leaves another man with none, and polygyny in practice is confined to men of unusual wealth or standing. Henrich, Boyd, and Richerson report that among tropical African foraging peoples whose rules permit polygyny, the share of polygynous marriages runs from 3 to 20 percent (The Puzzle of Monogamous Marriage, 2012, section 3). Even where polygyny is allowed, most marriages are between one husband and one wife. "Most societies permit polygyny" and "most marriages are polygynous" are different claims, and the record supports only the first.

The distinction matters for more than accuracy. In the example above, most marriages remain monogamous, yet a moderate degree of polygyny leaves two men in five with no possibility of marrying. Henrich, Boyd, and Richerson review cross-national, historical, and longitudinal evidence that large pools of unmarried low-status men are associated with higher rates of crime and violence, and they concede that the cross-sectional data alone cannot prove that being unmarried causes crime, since men who are less likely to commit crimes may also be more likely to marry. The longitudinal studies they cite, which follow the same men through married and unmarried periods of their lives, are what carry the causal claim.

Their conclusion is that societies enforcing monogamy by norm or law keep that pool small, and that this group-level advantage explains why monogamous marriage, rare in the record, spread with the growth of large and complex societies. They trace the roots of the modern form to classical Greece and Rome and note that laws prohibiting polygyny arrived in Japan in 1880, China in 1953, India in 1955, and Nepal in 1963. The 15 percent of societies with monogamous marriage fall into two groups: small societies in marginal environments with little difference in status among men, and some of the largest and most successful ancient states, which imposed monogamy by law. A reader who wants to test the argument should ask what it would predict about a society that permits polygyny but in which almost no one practices it, and whether the evidence reviewed can tell that case apart from a strictly monogamous one.

📚 PART 5 · KINSHIP BEYOND THE HOUSEHOLD

Marriage creates not just a new household but a new web of kinship: in-laws, extended family, and the network of obligations and privileges that come with them. Kinship systems vary enormously in their scope, their obligations, and their rules. In societies organized around clans or lineages, kinship extends to hundreds or thousands of people who share a common ancestor and owe each other significant mutual obligations. In modern individualistic societies, kinship obligations are largely confined to the nuclear family and close relatives.

The sociological significance of kinship extends beyond personal obligation. Kinship systems are, among other things, systems for organizing economic life (who inherits, who works together, who can claim support in times of need), political life (who can hold office, who speaks for the group), and religious life (who participates in ancestor rituals, who is spiritually connected to whom). To study kinship is to study the infrastructure of social life in all of its dimensions.

📍 CASE IN POINT · THE AMERICAN FAMILY OF THE 1830s

Tocqueville’s report on American kinship begins with an absence: “In America the family, in the Roman and aristocratic signification of the word, does not exist.” Paternal authority ends early. “In America there is, strictly speaking, no adolescence: at the close of boyhood the man appears, and begins to trace out his own path.” Daughters are raised to the same independence: “Long before an American girl arrives at the age of marriage, her emancipation from maternal control begins.” Then the pattern reverses at the wedding: “In America the independence of woman is irrevocably lost in the bonds of matrimony.”

He ties every one of these observations to the condition of equality rather than to American character. The family he describes is the Roman and aristocratic family with its extended lineage and lifelong paternal authority subtracted, and he treats that subtraction as a consequence of the wider social structure. His evidence is his own travel and conversation; the text gives no figures.

Source: Tocqueville, Democracy in America II.3.8 to 10 (1840), trans. Henry Reeve (public domain).
📚 PART 6 · WHAT THIS EXPLANATION LEAVES OUT

Explaining marriage by the functions it performs is useful, and it is also vulnerable to a real objection. If you explain an arrangement by the social function it serves, you can end up sounding as though you have justified it, including the parts of it that were unjust. Ancient property law that treated a wife as her husband's dependent performed a function too, in the narrow sense that it organized inheritance predictably. A functional explanation on its own cannot tell you whether an arrangement was fair to the people living inside it.

A functionalist sociologist has an answer, and it is worth stating precisely rather than dismissing the objection. The claim was never that a function makes an arrangement good. The claim is narrower: that an arrangement persisting across history and across unrelated cultures is unlikely to be arbitrary, and the function it performs is part of any honest explanation of why it persisted. That a marriage system regulated property in ways that disadvantaged women is a separate, and answerable, question of justice. Sociology can show what an institution does without settling whether it should keep doing it.

▶  In your notebook, name one arrangement in your own family or community that looks like a straightforward personal choice but is actually doing one of the four functions above. Then open this.
There is no single right answer here, but a useful test is the one Durkheim would apply: would this arrangement have looked different if you had grown up in a different family, or is it something almost every family around you also does, in roughly the same way? An arrangement that varies wildly from household to household is closer to a personal choice. An arrangement that looks strikingly similar across many households you did not grow up in is doing social work, whatever it feels like from the inside.

💪 WHAT YOU NOW KNOW

Marriage looks like the most private decision a person makes, and in every documented society it is also one of the most regulated. Both things are true at once, and the regulation is not evidence against the feeling. It is evidence that even the most intimate human bonds carry social weight: property, descent, obligation, and the shape of the next generation all run through the household. Wednesday takes the same move, personal on the surface and structural underneath, and applies it to something that feels even more private than marriage: what a person believes.

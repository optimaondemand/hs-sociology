WHAT TO READ
This reading is in a book, not on this page. Here is exactly where to start and stop.
THE BOOK
Emile Durkheim, The Division of Labour in Society. Translated by George Simpson. Any printing of the Simpson translation works. If your library holds a different translation, that is fine too, but the chapter titles below are the Simpson ones.
START AT
Book I, Chapter II, titled Mechanical Solidarity Through Likeness. The chapter opens: “The link of social solidarity to which repressive law corresponds…”
STOP AT
The end of Book I, Chapter III, titled Solidarity Arising from the Division of Labour, or Organic Solidarity. Chapter II ends with the words “…bears its mark”; continue straight on through Chapter III to its end.
HOW LONG
Two chapters, roughly forty to fifty pages depending on your printing. Set aside about ninety minutes and read it in two sittings if that suits you better.
WHAT TO SKIP
Nothing, but do not slow down for Durkheim's long surveys of crime across different legal systems. Those are evidence for a claim you will already have understood. Read them quickly and keep moving.
Page numbers are useless here because they differ between printings, which is why the boundaries above are given by chapter and by the words themselves. If the first words you find do not match, you are in the wrong chapter.

💡 WHERE THIS FITS
Monday's lesson gave you mechanical and organic solidarity as a finished distinction. These two chapters are where Durkheim builds it, and the order matters: he does not start from society at all. He starts from punishment, works backward to what punishment protects, and only then names the kind of solidarity that must exist for punishment to make sense. Watch the direction of the reasoning.
📖 WHY YOU ARE READING THIS

This is the text where the mechanical and organic distinction is introduced. Durkheim is arguing something counterintuitive: that specialisation creates solidarity rather than dissolving it. Most people, then and now, assume the opposite, which is why he spends two chapters on it.

Chapter II moves in five steps. Track them as you read and the argument holds together.

1
Start from crime, not from society. A crime is whatever provokes punishment. So asking what crime is means asking what punishment is for.
2
Note that the reaction is always the same. Across wildly different societies and legal systems, crimes differ but the response does not. Unity of effect points to unity of cause.
3
Ask what is being offended. Not every strong sentiment is protected by penal law. Filial piety is strong and vague; penal rules are strong and precise. Precision is the distinguishing mark.
4
Name the thing. The shared, precise, common beliefs form a system with a life of its own, and Durkheim gives it a name.
5
Reverse the usual view of punishment. Punishment is not mainly for the criminal at all. Watch for who Durkheim says it is really aimed at. This is the surprise of the chapter.
🎯 READ FOR THESE FOUR QUESTIONS
  1. Durkheim argues that the division of labour produces solidarity. What is the mechanism by which specialisation creates social bonds rather than dissolving them?
  2. What is the collective conscience, and how does its strength vary between simple and complex societies? What does that change imply for law and punishment?
  3. Durkheim distinguishes normal from abnormal forms of the division of labour. What makes a division of labour abnormal, and what conditions produce anomie?
  4. He wrote this as a doctoral thesis, arguing against a liberalism which held that modern industrial societies could run on contract alone without shared moral norms. How does his argument challenge that view?
🔍 THREE SENTENCES TO FIND AND MARK

Three sentences carry Chapter II. Find each one in your copy and mark it, then read the note underneath. Everything else in the chapter is argument in support of these.

1. The definition. After ruling out the alternatives, Durkheim names his central concept:

“The totality of beliefs and sentiments common to average citizens of the same society forms a determinate system which has its own life; one may call it the collective or common conscience.”

Book I, Chapter II

Read has its own life slowly. That phrase is doing the work of an entire chapter of The Rules of Sociological Method, which you read last week. He is claiming the collective conscience is a real thing with its own properties, not a convenient way of summarising what individuals happen to believe. Notice also average citizens, which is how he keeps the concept measurable rather than mystical.

2. The conclusion it supports. Having defined the term, he can finally answer the question the chapter opened with:

“An act is criminal when it offends strong and defined states of the collective conscience.”

Book I, Chapter II

Both adjectives are load-bearing and neither is decoration. Strong rules out trivial preferences. Defined rules out vague aspirations like charity or filial love, which are deeply felt and never prosecuted. Test the definition against a case: why is being a bad son not a crime, while damaging a fence is? Durkheim's answer is precision, not intensity.

3. The reversal. Late in the chapter he says what punishment is actually for, and it is not what most readers expect:

“Its true function is to maintain social cohesion intact, while maintaining all its vitality in the common conscience.”

Book I, Chapter II

Durkheim has already conceded that punishment works poorly at reforming offenders and poorly at deterring others. He is not being cynical. He is saying we have misunderstood the target. Read on a few lines and you will find him state directly who punishment is designed to act upon, and the answer is not the criminal. When you find that sentence, write it in your journal. It is the single most surprising claim in the chapter.

✏️ AFTER YOU READ

Write short answers to the four questions in your Sociological Journal document.

▶  Write down who you think punishment is really aimed at, then open this.
Durkheim says punishment is above all designed to act upon upright people, meaning the ones who did nothing wrong. His reasoning is that a shared moral belief which is publicly violated and then met with nothing would quietly lose its force. Punishment is the community's way of saying, at the moment of contradiction, that the belief still holds. On this account the criminal is almost incidental to the ceremony. That is a genuinely strange idea, and you should decide whether you accept it. One test: does it explain why people who will never be victims still want to see wrongdoers punished, and why a punishment that seems too lenient upsets bystanders who lost nothing?
▶  Answer question 1 in your notebook first, then open this.
The mechanism is dependence. Specialising means giving up the ability to meet your own needs, and what you give up, someone else must supply. Multiply that across a whole population and you have a society whose members cannot survive apart, which is a bond whether or not anyone experiences it as one. Notice that this bond needs no shared belief at all, which is exactly what makes the claim strong and also what makes it vulnerable. If interdependence alone were enough, Durkheim would not need the collective conscience, and Chapter III would not spend so long worrying about what happens when the division of labour goes wrong. He is arguing against people who thought contract alone could do it. He is not saying structure alone can either.

📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Why does a city of three million strangers not fall apart?
Durkheim's answer is counterintuitive enough that it is worth stating as a claim to be tested rather than a fact to accept. He argues that people become more tightly bound to one another as they become less alike. If that is true, it reverses what most people assume about modern life.

📚 PART 1 · WHY DOESN'T IT FALL APART?
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Before you read: what do you think actually keeps a large modern city from breaking down? (No wrong answer. You are about to meet four rival answers, and one of them is probably yours.)

Imagine a city of three million people. No single person knows more than a few hundred of them. They do not share the same religion, speak the same language at home, or agree on politics. They are strangers to one another in almost every sense. And yet they do not tear each other apart. Goods arrive at markets. Laws are mostly followed. Children go to school. Fires are put out. Bills are paid. The city functions.

How?

This is not a trivial question. It is arguably the central question of social theory, and the thinkers you have already met each answered it. Hobbes thought the answer was power, in the form of a sovereign strong enough to frighten everyone into cooperation. Locke and Rousseau looked to contract, meaning rational individuals agreeing to terms. Tocqueville pointed to voluntary associations, civic habits, and the culture of local participation. Each answer captures something real.

Durkheim's answer is different and more structural. Societies cohere, he argues, because they develop forms of solidarity appropriate to their level of complexity, meaning bonds of mutual dependence and shared identity that match how the society is actually organised. The kind of solidarity holding a small village together is not the kind holding an industrial city together. Understanding that difference is the work of this lesson.

📚 PART 2 · THE TERMS
Solidarity
From the Latin solidus, solid or whole. Social cohesion: the bonds that unite individuals into a functioning society. Durkheim's question is what those bonds are made of and how they change.
Mechanical solidarity
Cohesion based on likeness. People are bound together because they are similar, sharing beliefs, practices, customs, and a way of life.
Organic solidarity
Cohesion based on interdependence. People are bound together precisely because they are different, each performing a specialised function the others depend on.
Division of labour
The specialisation of economic and social roles across a population. In a modern economy nobody produces everything they need.
Collective conscience (conscience collective)
The body of shared beliefs, values, and moral sentiments common to the members of a society. Not any individual's conscience but the group's. Recall from Week 3 that the French word carries both conscience and consciousness.
Anomie
From the Greek a-nomos, without law or norm. A condition of normlessness in which the social norms that give people purpose, direction, and belonging break down. Durkheim treats it as a pathology of modern society, not an inevitability.

📚 PART 4 · TWO WAYS TO HOLD A SOCIETY TOGETHER

THE COST OF THE SECOND FORM
Anomie. When people are bound by need rather than shared belief, the moral framework that tells them what to want and what to expect can thin out with the collective conscience. Durkheim does not treat this as the price of progress. He treats it as a pathology, which means he thinks it can be treated.

The society of resemblance

In The Division of Labour in Society (1893), Durkheim begins by asking what bond holds the simplest societies together, and his answer is sameness. In a small traditional community, whether a village, a tribe, or an ancient city-state, most people live similar lives. They perform similar work, worship the same gods, observe the same festivals, speak the same language without significant variation, and absorb the same moral code from childhood. The collective conscience is strong and all-encompassing. There is little room for significant individual deviation, because the group's identity depends on its members being alike in the relevant respects.

He calls this mechanical solidarity, and the word needs care. He does not mean unfeeling or automatic. He means that the members cohere the way the parts of a simple machine cohere, by fitting the same template. The bond is powerful, and it is a bond of similarity. A person who deviates sharply from such a community's shared beliefs does not merely make an unusual choice. She threatens the solidarity of the whole, which is why deviance in traditional societies tends to be punished with unusual severity.

The legal system reflects this. Crimes are answered not primarily to repair harm or rehabilitate the offender but to reaffirm the collective conscience the crime violated. The punishment is expressive. It announces, on behalf of the community, that this is not who we are.

The society of interdependence

Modern industrial societies look nothing like this. Their members are not similar. They are spectacularly different. A modern city contains surgeons and janitors, software engineers and farmers, priests and atheists, speakers of dozens of languages and practitioners of hundreds of distinct cultural traditions. The collective conscience, in any strong sense, has thinned. Shared religious belief, which once supplied the moral cement of European societies, no longer commands universal assent. What holds such a society together?

Durkheim's answer is the division of labour, and this is the counterintuitive move. Precisely because modern people are specialised, they depend on each other in ways people in simple societies do not. The surgeon cannot grow her own food. The farmer cannot perform his own surgery. Neither can manufacture the equipment they use, adjudicate their own legal disputes, or educate their own children to the level modern society requires. Each person needs an entire social system in order to live, and that need creates bonds. Not bonds of likeness, but bonds of mutual dependence.

This is organic solidarity, and the analogy is to a living body in which different organs perform different functions and each requires the others to survive. No single organ is the body. Each is a specialised part. The body's unity is not the unity of identical cells but the unity of coordinated difference. The law changes accordingly. Modern law is less concerned with punishing violations of shared belief than with restoring what has been disrupted: compensating the injured party, adjudicating disputes, and maintaining the conditions under which complex interdependence can keep working. It is restitutive rather than expressive.

📚 PART 5 · THE PROBLEM OF ANOMIE

If organic solidarity answers how modern societies cohere, Durkheim is also honest about its fragility. The collective conscience of a modern society is thin. When people are bound by interdependence rather than shared belief, the moral framework that tells them what their lives mean can weaken or disappear. That framework is what tells a person what they owe others, what to aspire to, and what limits to observe. Durkheim calls the condition of its absence anomie.

In Suicide (1897) he shows that anomie is not merely a philosophical concept but a measurable social pathology. Suicide rates rise during periods of rapid economic change, and here is the detail that makes the finding serious: they rise not only in downturns but in booms. Rapid change of any direction disrupts the norms that regulate desire. When the rules of social life are clear, people know what to want and what to expect. When those rules dissolve, desire becomes unlimited and therefore impossible to satisfy. The resulting suffering is not physical but social, and it belongs to a person with no sense of where they fit or what they are for.

Anomie is the shadow side of modernity. A society that frees individuals from the strong collective conscience of mechanical solidarity also risks leaving them without the moorings that make a human life coherent.

▶  In your notebook: why does it matter to Durkheim's argument that suicide rates rise in booms as well as slumps? Then open this.
Because it rules out the obvious rival explanation. If rates rose only in downturns, anyone could say the cause is hardship, and no sociology would be needed to explain why misery follows poverty. Rates rising in prosperity cannot be explained that way. Something other than material suffering has to be doing the work, and Durkheim's candidate is the disruption of norms, which happens in both directions. This is the same argumentative move you saw in Week 3. He does not pick the easy case. He picks the case that only his explanation survives. When you assess any sociological claim, look for whether the evidence rules out the rivals or merely fits the favoured one.
📚 PART 6 · DURKHEIM AND TOCQUEVILLE

Both thinkers are responding to the same historical fact: the emergence of a modern, individualistic, democratic society from the ruins of an older, hierarchical, communal one. They approach it from different angles and arrive at remedies that are close cousins.

Tocqueville's worry is that democratic individualism tends toward a quiet withdrawal from public life, which he calls soft despotism, and his remedy is associational life. Durkheim shares the concern about fragmentation but analyses its causes at a more structural level. Where Tocqueville looks at political habits and civic culture, Durkheim looks at the division of labour and the collective conscience. Where Tocqueville's remedy is civic participation, Durkheim's is the cultivation of professional and occupational associations, which are intermediate institutions standing between the individual and the state and capable of supplying normative structure in a differentiated society.

Notice what they agree about. Both see institutions as the answer. They disagree about which institutions matter most, and that disagreement is worth holding, because Weeks 7, 8, and 17 are largely an argument about it.

💪 WHAT YOU NOW KNOW

The institutions this course keeps returning to are family, religion, education, and voluntary associations, and they are not incidental to social life. They are the machinery by which solidarity is produced and reproduced, and they are what stands between a functioning society and the anomic condition Durkheim describes. Studying them carefully is how you finally answer the question this lesson opened with: how the city of three million strangers manages, against considerable odds, to hold together.

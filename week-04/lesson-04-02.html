📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What does a city do to the mind of the person living in it?
Durkheim asked what holds a society together. Simmel, born the same year, asked something narrower and stranger: what is it actually like in there? His answers are unusually hard to summarise, because he refuses to say whether the modern city is good or bad for us. Resist the urge to make him choose.

📚 PART 1 · A CITY THAT GREW ELEVEN TIMES OVER

Georg Simmel was born in Berlin in 1858, the same year as Durkheim, and spent most of his career in that city. Berlin in the late nineteenth century was expanding at a rate with no precedent in human history. In 1800 it held 170,000 people. By 1900 it held 1.9 million. Within a single lifetime the city had gone from a modest Prussian capital to one of the great industrial metropolises of the world. Simmel watched the transformation and asked what it was doing to the human beings living through it.

His answer, published as "The Metropolis and Mental Life" in 1903, turns on what he calls the blasé attitude. A person in a modern city is met at every moment with more stimulation, impression, and demand for response than any nervous system was built to handle. To survive it, the urban person learns to screen. She becomes selective, detached, reserved. She does not respond to the face of every stranger she passes, because she cannot. The personality that results is shaped by the city itself: quick, guarded, competent, and cool.

HOW THE CITY PRODUCES A PERSONALITY
Simmel's argument is causal, not disapproving. The blasé attitude is an adaptation, and adaptations have costs.
THE CONDITION
Stimulus beyond capacity
Faces, signs, noise, traffic, transactions, demands for attention, arriving faster than any nervous system evolved to process them.
→
THE ADAPTATION
Screening
The mind learns to filter. Most of what arrives is not responded to at all. This is not rudeness. It is the only way to remain functional.
→
THE RESULT
The blasé attitude
A settled incapacity for strong response. Intellect takes over from feeling, because intellect is the faculty that can handle volume.
What it protects. Without it a person in a metropolis would be overwhelmed within an hour. It makes urban life survivable, and with it the freedom, anonymity, and possibility a city offers.
What it costs. The filter does not discriminate perfectly. Things worth responding to are screened out alongside the noise, and the capacity for strong response, once dulled, is not easily recovered.
📚 PART 2 · FOUR TERMS

Tap each to check yourself, then write all four into your notebook in your own words. You will need the fourth again next week.

⚡ TAP TO REVEAL
Blasé attitude
The emotional detachment characteristic of urban life. An incapacity for strong emotional response to city stimuli, produced by the need to manage an overwhelming volume of impressions.
⚡ TAP TO REVEAL
Money economy
An economic system in which goods and services are exchanged through money rather than through direct barter or obligation. For Simmel it transforms social relationships by making them calculable, impersonal, and abstract.
⚡ TAP TO REVEAL
Formal sociology
Simmel's distinctive approach. Rather than studying particular societies or institutions, he studies the recurring forms of social interaction, such as competition, conflict, exchange, domination, and subordination, which appear across quite different historical contexts.
⚡ TAP TO REVEAL
The stranger
From Simmel's 1908 essay. A person simultaneously near and far, part of a group but not of it. The stranger sees clearly what insiders cannot, because she has not been absorbed into the group's unexamined assumptions. You will read this essay next week.
📚 PART 3 · TWO CONTEMPORARIES, TWO DISCIPLINES

Simmel and Durkheim are exact contemporaries and in some ways rivals. Both ask what happens to individuals and communities in modern industrial society. They ask it differently, and the difference produced two distinct traditions.

DurkheimSimmel
Object of studySociety as a structural reality, and the forces that hold it together or let it dissolve.The texture of interaction, and what it actually feels like to live inside modern society.
MethodTreat social facts as things. Count them where possible.Isolate the recurring forms of interaction, wherever they appear.
Central problemAnomie: the weakening of the norms that give life purpose and belonging.The tragedy of culture, which is subtler and harder to measure.
Characteristic moveFind a rate and explain it socially.Find a form and describe it precisely.

The tragedy of culture is worth pausing on. Simmel means that the objective products of human culture, meaning cities, institutions, technologies, and art forms, grow so complex and so autonomous that they outrun any individual's capacity to absorb them or be genuinely animated by them. The modern person is surrounded by achievements she did not make and cannot fully understand. She is, in a real sense, a stranger inside her own civilisation.

📚 PART 4 · WHAT MONEY DOES TO A RELATIONSHIP

One of Simmel's most important contributions is his analysis of the money economy in The Philosophy of Money (1900). Money is not merely a convenient medium of exchange. He argues that it is a force which transforms the character of social life itself.

In a society organised by personal obligation and local relationship, exchanging goods is inseparable from the social bond between the parties. You give your neighbour a bushel of grain and she gives you a day of labour. The exchange is personal, qualitative, and embedded in an ongoing web of relationship. Money dissolves all of that. With money, every good and service becomes quantifiable and comparable, every transaction is between equivalents, and the parties need no relationship to each other beyond the transaction itself. Money is the great leveller and the great depersonaliser.

The consequence is psychological as well as economic. People living in a money economy come to think quantitatively, asking of everything what it is worth in money. That habit is extraordinarily useful for coordinating complex social life. Simmel also thinks it corrodes the qualitative dimensions of experience, because the things that cannot be priced are progressively displaced from the centre of social life by the things that can. Beauty, loyalty, love, and sacred obligation do not disappear. They simply stop being what the society is organised around.

⚡ TRY IT · WHICH CONCEPT IS AT WORK?
Each situation below is best explained by one of Simmel's four ideas. Choose the one that does the most explanatory work, not merely one that could apply.
1. A neighbour who used to help with repairs for free begins charging an hourly rate, and both of them find the relationship has changed in a way neither likes.
2. Someone who has commuted through a crowded station for years no longer registers the faces around her and could not describe a single one.
3. A newcomer to a small firm keeps noticing inefficiencies that everyone else stopped seeing years ago, and her colleagues find this both useful and slightly irritating.
4. A researcher argues that the pattern of rivalry between two shopkeepers, two siblings, and two nations is recognisably the same pattern in three different settings.
5. Someone deciding whether to take up painting finds themselves calculating whether the hours could be better spent, and cannot stop framing the question that way.
📍 CASE IN POINT · BERLIN AND LONDON AROUND 1900

Simmel wrote about the city he lived in. His test of how far the money economy had reorganized daily life is a thought experiment about clocks: if every watch in Berlin drifted out of step, even by as little as an hour, the city’s whole economic and commercial life would stall for a time. Punctuality, in his account, is not a personal virtue of Berliners but a requirement of a place where millions of differentiated interests intertwine and must mesh on schedule.

For London he borrows a remark from an English constitutional historian he does not name: that across English history London has served less as the nation’s heart than as its mind, and at every period as its purse. He offers it as illustration rather than proof. What in the Berlin example depends on money specifically, and what would hold in any large city?

Source: Simmel, “The Metropolis and Mental Life” (1903), opening sections; paraphrased from the German original.
📚 PART 5 · THE STRANGER, AND THE SOCIOLOGIST

Simmel's essay "The Stranger" (1908) shows his method at its best. He is not analysing any particular historical stranger but the social form of strangeness itself, meaning the structural position of a person who is simultaneously inside and outside a group. The stranger brings goods from a distance the group cannot produce for itself. She sees what insiders cannot, because she has not been formed by the group's unexamined assumptions. For both reasons she is useful and threatening at once. Many historical groups assigned strangers to particular occupations, among them trading, money-lending, and medicine, precisely because outsider status gave useful access to what insiders could not see.

The concept matters beyond the history of particular marginalised groups, because it describes the position of the sociologist. Like Simmel's stranger, the sociologist's task is to see what insiders take for granted and to make visible the social forms organising everyday life that participants never recognise as forms at all. In this respect Simmel, Durkheim, and Tocqueville are all describing one intellectual posture: the deliberate cultivation of distance from your own social world as the condition of understanding it.

▶  Decide in your notebook: is the modern city, on balance, liberating or imprisoning? Then open this.
Simmel ends the essay by raising this question and declining to settle it, and that is a position rather than an evasion. The city grants a freedom no village could: anonymity, the possibility of becoming someone other than who your neighbours expect, access to people and ideas a small community cannot supply. It exacts a price in the same motion, because the anonymity that frees you also means nobody is watching out for you, and the blasé attitude that makes the crowd bearable also dulls the responsiveness a full life needs. Simmel's discipline is to hold both without collapsing into either nostalgia or celebration. If your answer came out cleanly one way, check whether you reached it by ignoring half the evidence. That check is the skill this lesson is really teaching.

💪 WHAT YOU NOW KNOW

Durkheim gave you a structure and Simmel has given you a texture. Both were looking at the same Europe in the same decades and they built different disciplines out of it, which is worth remembering whenever a single framework starts to feel like the whole truth. Simmel's particular gift is his refusal to resolve. The city liberates and it costs, the blasé attitude protects and it dulls, money coordinates and it flattens. Holding two true things that pull against each other is harder than picking one, and it is closer to how social life actually works.

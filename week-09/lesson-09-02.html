📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
If a government rules by force alone, how long can it last, and why?
Every society that has ever existed has had some form of government, some arrangement for making binding decisions and some authority for enforcing them. That much is close to a sociological universal. Why people go along with it is not.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain why sociologists distinguish authority from mere power, and why that distinction predicts which governments last.
2
I will be able to reconstruct Aristotle's account of the state as natural against the social contract tradition's account of it as artificial, and say what is actually at stake in the disagreement.
3
I will be able to apply Durkheim's distinction between repressive and restitutive law to a case outside the lesson, and explain what the case reveals about the kind of solidarity behind it.
📚 PART 1 · WHY DO WE OBEY?
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
A new law is passed that almost nobody likes. Predict: what actually makes most people follow it day to day, once the news coverage fades? (No wrong answer.)

Why do people obey? Why does the citizen pay her taxes, the soldier follow his orders, the subject bow before the king? The obvious answer is force, because if she does not, something bad will happen to her. That answer is not wrong, but it is incomplete. Governments that rule entirely by force are expensive, fragile, and exhausting. The most durable and effective political arrangements are those in which most people obey most of the time not merely because they fear punishment but because they believe in the legitimacy of the authority they are obeying.

The concept of legitimacy is one of the central preoccupations of political sociology. Legitimate authority is authority that those subject to it believe is rightfully exercised, a claim that has a valid hold on obedience and not merely the power to demand it. Understanding what makes authority legitimate, and what undermines that legitimacy, is essential to understanding political order and political collapse alike.

KEY TERMS
Authority
From the Latin auctoritas, power derived from one's standing or expertise. Power regarded as rightful and legitimate by those subject to it. Authority differs from mere power in that it commands compliance through recognized right, not only through force.
Legitimacy
The quality of being recognized as rightful and proper. Political legitimacy is the belief, on the part of those governed, that the governing authority has a valid claim to their compliance.
Law
A system of rules enforced by a political authority with the power to impose sanctions on violators. Law differs from more informal norms in its formal codification, its systematic enforcement, and its explicit connection to political power.
State
Weber's definition: a political organization that claims a monopoly on the legitimate use of physical force within a given territory. Modern states are distinguished from earlier political arrangements by their degree of bureaucratic organization and their claim to territorial sovereignty.
📚 PART 2 · THE HISTORICAL DEVELOPMENT OF GOVERNMENT

Aristotle argued that government is natural. The polis emerges from the household and the village as naturally as a mature plant emerges from a seed. Humans are political animals, and the state is not an external imposition on human nature but its fullest expression. Aristotle states the claim at Politics I.2 (Week 1): the state is a creation of nature, and man is by nature a political animal. This view stands in direct contrast to the social contract tradition of Hobbes, Locke, and Rousseau, all of whom treated the state as an artificial construction, a device created by rational, or at least calculating, individuals to solve the coordination problems that would otherwise make life miserable.

From a sociological standpoint, neither of these accounts is primarily an empirical claim about how governments actually originated in history. Both are normative accounts. Each is attempting to explain what justifies political authority, not how it historically emerged. The historical record itself suggests early states emerged through a combination of conquest, coordination, and gradually legitimized tradition.

The content of legitimating claims changes. The need for legitimacy persists.

Chieftains became kings; kings became dynasties; dynasties claimed divine sanction; divine sanction became constitutional authority; constitutional authority became democratic legitimacy. Notice what does not change across five very different eras. Every single arrangement on that ladder still had to answer the same question Aristotle and the social contract theorists were both asking from opposite directions: on what ground does this power have a right to be obeyed?

📍 CASE IN POINT · THE EARLY GREEKS IN ARISTOTLE’S ACCOUNT OF KINGSHIP

Aristotle’s account of how government arises is anchored in a claim about his own people’s past. “Hellenic states were originally governed by kings; because the Hellenes were under royal rule before they came together, as the barbarians still are. Every family is ruled by the eldest, and therefore in the colonies of the family the kingly form of government prevailed because they were of the same blood.” Kingship, on this account, is the household’s rule by the eldest carried over into the village, and the village into the city.

Two things about the evidence. His witness for the earliest stage is Homer, whom he quotes: “Each one gives law to his children and to his wives.” And the word rendered “barbarians” is Aristotle’s term for all non-Greek-speaking peoples, used here to mark a stage he believes the Greeks had passed through, not a description of any one society he had studied. Which parts of the sequence are argument, and which are observation?

Source: Aristotle, Politics I.2 (c. 350 B.C.), trans. Benjamin Jowett (public domain).
📚 PART 3 · LAW AS A SOCIAL INSTITUTION

Durkheim argued that the form of law in any society reflects its form of social solidarity. The distinction is mechanical against organic solidarity (Week 4); here it drives two very different kinds of legal system.

Repressive lawRestitutive law
What it doesPunishes harshly, to reaffirm the collective conscience.Compensates and repairs, to restore a disrupted relationship.
Solidarity typeMechanical. The collective conscience is strong and shared.Organic. Individuals depend on each other through specialised roles.
What a violation meansAn offense against the whole community's shared beliefs.A broken arrangement between specific, interdependent parties.
CitationDivision of Labour I.2Division of Labour I.3

As societies modernize, their legal systems tend to become more elaborate, more specialized, and more oriented toward restitution than toward collective moral expression. This does not mean modern law has nothing to do with morality. It means the moral dimension of law in modern societies is more complex and more contested than it was in traditional ones, precisely because organic solidarity depends on interdependence between people who no longer share one collective conscience in the way a small traditional society did.

📚 PART 4 · TEST IT: FORCE OR LEGITIMACY?

Place each case on the spectrum.

⚡ TRY IT · PLACE EACH CASE
The question is not whether force is present at all, since it almost always is somewhere in the background. The question is what is actually doing the work of producing obedience in this particular case.
1. A driver stops at a red light on an empty road at two in the morning, with no camera or officer anywhere near, simply because running it feels wrong.
2. Villagers hand over half their harvest to an occupying warlord's soldiers, who have already burned the last house that refused.
3. A citizen pays a newly legislated tax she strongly opposes but does not attempt to evade, because the legislature that passed it was elected fairly.
4. A factory worker follows a safety rule she considers pointless, mainly because inspectors fine the plant heavily whenever a violation is found.
5. An office keeps a decades-old seating chart nobody can explain, because that is simply where people sit.
▶  Could a government survive on force alone for a long stretch of time?
The lesson's claim is not that force never works, but that it is expensive to sustain. A government that relies on force alone has to fund and staff enforcement everywhere, all the time, with no failures, because the moment enforcement lapses anywhere, compliance there has no other reason to continue. A government most people obey because they believe it has a right to rule needs far less machinery, because belief does the enforcing work that soldiers would otherwise have to do. That is an empirical claim about cost and durability, not a moral claim about which governments deserve to survive.

💪 WHAT YOU NOW KNOW

Government is old, and the question of what makes it legitimate is older than any particular answer to it. Aristotle located legitimacy in whether rule serves the common good; the social contract tradition located it in consent; Durkheim showed that even something as procedural as law carries the fingerprint of the kind of solidarity that produced it. What all three share is the assumption this lesson opened with: force can compel a body, but only a belief in legitimacy can produce the kind of steady, low-cost obedience that lets a political order actually last.

📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
You have read what nine sociologists concluded. How did any of them know?
Every claim you have studied this quarter rests on a method: a way of gathering evidence and a way of arguing from it. Before you carry these tools into your own observation of a real institution, you need to know what each method can and cannot show, and, just as important, where a claim stops being a fact and starts being an interpretation.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to distinguish the major sociological research methods and state a genuine strength and a genuine limitation of each.
2
I will be able to explain the difference between what a body of data shows and what a sociologist claims it means.
3
I will be able to evaluate a sociological claim by identifying what evidence would count against it.
📚 PART 1 · HOW SOCIOLOGY KNOWS WHAT IT KNOWS
Survey
STRENGTHGeneralizes across large populations from a representative sample.
LIMITMisses the texture of lived experience behind the numbers.

STRENGTHSustained observation captures rich, qualitative detail.
LIMITCannot generalize beyond the group actually observed.

STRENGTHIdentifies patterns across different periods or societies.

⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
A sociologist wants to show that a decline in trust between neighbors is a real, patterned social phenomenon, not just a vivid impression drawn from a few loud examples. Predict: which approach would you reach for first? (No wrong answer.)

Sociology is a science, not in the sense that it achieves the mathematical certainty of physics, but in the sense that it subjects claims about social life to systematic empirical testing. This means that sociological knowledge is not merely opinion, not merely ideology, and not merely common sense. It means that sociological claims can be examined, challenged, and refined in the light of evidence. It means that a sociologist who wishes to claim that social capital is declining must produce evidence for the claim, not merely assert it.

The major methods of sociological research include surveys, structured questionnaires administered to representative samples, allowing generalization to populations; ethnography, sustained participant observation of a social group in its natural setting, producing rich qualitative description; historical comparative analysis, the systematic comparison of social patterns across different historical periods or societies; and experiment, rare in sociology, but used in laboratory and field settings to test causal claims. Each method has a characteristic strength and a characteristic limitation, as the cards above show.

Durkheim's study of suicide is the paradigmatic example of the survey method. Berger's ethnographic sensibility represents the qualitative tradition, learned through the readings substituted in his place this quarter. Tocqueville's Democracy in America is a masterpiece of historical comparative analysis. The best sociological work typically combines methods, using quantitative patterns to identify what needs to be explained, and qualitative depth to explain it.

📚 PART 2 · FACT AND INTERPRETATION IN SOCIOLOGICAL DATA

One of the most important intellectual skills a sociology student can develop is the ability to distinguish between what the data shows and what a sociologist claims it means. Putnam's data on declining bowling league membership is a fact: the numbers come from documented records. His claim that this decline represents declining social capital is an interpretation, a theoretical framing that other sociologists have contested. His further claim that declining social capital threatens democratic health draws on Tocqueville and Nisbet and requires its own evidence. Reading sociology critically means tracking the movement from data to interpretation and asking, at each step, whether the inference is warranted, what alternative interpretations are possible, and what evidence would change your mind.

💪 WHAT YOU NOW KNOW

Every argument you have studied this quarter rests on a method, and every method has a place where fact ends and interpretation begins. Knowing where that seam runs is not a technicality. It is the difference between using a thinker's framework and simply repeating his conclusions. When you observe a real institution for the Community Institution Study, you will be doing what Berger and Tocqueville did before you: recording what you actually saw, and then arguing, carefully, for what it means.

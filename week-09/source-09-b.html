WHAT TO READ
This reading is in a book, not on this page. Here is exactly where to start.
THE BOOK
Max Weber, The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism. Translated by Talcott Parsons, 1930. Any printing of the Parsons translation works.
START AT
The very beginning of the Author's Introduction, the short essay that opens the book, before Chapter I. It opens: “A product of modern European civilization, studying any problem of universal history…”
READ TO
Weber's definition of capitalism as the pursuit of continuous, rational profit, which closes with the words “…would be doomed to extinction.” That is roughly two-thirds of the Introduction. If your copy continues past that point into a discussion of the "spirit" of capitalism specifically, you may keep reading, but nothing past this point is required for this week's guide questions.
HOW LONG
About seven to nine pages depending on your printing. Twenty-five to thirty-five minutes at a careful pace. This is denser than most of the course's readings; do not rush it.
WHAT TO SKIP
Nothing within the assigned span. Weber's survey moves quickly through science, law, art, architecture, and the state before reaching capitalism; each example is doing real work, so read the list rather than skimming it.
Page numbers are useless here because they differ between printings, which is why the boundaries above are given by structure and by the words themselves. If the words you find at the start do not match, you are in the wrong section of the book.

💡 WHERE THIS FITS
Wednesday's lesson asked how sociology knows what it knows, and named the historical comparative method as one of the discipline's major tools. This is that method used on the largest question Weber ever asked: why the modern rational economic and political order arose in the West, and nowhere else, even though other civilizations matched or exceeded Europe in almost every particular skill he can name. Weber is also the thinker Quarter 2 leans on most heavily, so this reading is a bridge as much as a synthesis.
📖 WHY YOU ARE READING THIS

Weber opens with a puzzle: many civilizations produced brilliant science, art, and law, yet only the West produced the specific combination of rational science, rational law, a rational bureaucratic state, and rational capitalism that now organizes the whole modern world. He is not claiming the West is simply more advanced. He is asking a much more careful question: what, precisely, is different about the Western form of these achievements, and why did that specific form appear only here?

The Introduction moves in five steps. Track them as you read.

1
Pose the comparative question. Why did the line of cultural development we call "universal" appear only in Western civilization?
2
Rule out the easy answer, science. Empirical knowledge existed everywhere, above all in India, China, Babylonia, and Egypt. What was missing elsewhere was not knowledge but a specific rational, systematic method.
3
Show the pattern repeats. Music, architecture, law, and the state each show the same shape: sophisticated achievement everywhere, but a distinctively rational, systematized form only in the West.
4
Name the trained official. The state itself, run by specially trained officials under written, rational law, is a Western invention with no full precedent elsewhere.
5
Reject the naive definition of capitalism. Greed for money is universal and ancient. Watch for what Weber says capitalism actually is, and how sharply it differs from mere greed.
🎯 READ FOR THESE FOUR QUESTIONS
  1. What specifically is Weber trying to explain? Why is the question interesting, and why is it not sufficient to say that capitalism developed in the West because Western countries were richer or more advanced?
  2. What does Weber mean by "rationalization" as a distinctive feature of Western culture? How does it differ from what he finds in other great civilizations?
  3. Weber explicitly says he is not arguing that religion causes economics in a simple way. What is his more nuanced claim about the relationship between religious ideas and economic behavior?
  4. How does the comparative method Weber uses here differ from the statistical method Durkheim uses in Suicide? What can each method discover that the other cannot?
🔍 THREE SENTENCES TO FIND AND MARK

Three sentences carry the structure of the Introduction. Find each one in your copy and mark it, then read the note underneath.

1. The question itself. In the opening paragraph, Weber states exactly what needs explaining:

“…cultural phenomena have appeared which (as we like to think) lie in a line of development having universal significance and value.”

Author's Introduction, opening paragraph

Notice the parenthetical, as we like to think. Weber is not simply asserting Western superiority. He is naming an assumption his own civilization makes about itself, and then setting out to test whether the assumption survives careful comparison. That is a very different project from cultural boosterism, and it is worth deciding for yourself, by the end of the essay, whether he keeps that promise.

2. Rationalization, in miniature. After surveying science, art, and architecture, Weber makes the same point about the modern official and the modern state:

“…a rational, systematic, and specialized pursuit of science, with trained and specialized personnel, has only existed in the West in a sense at all approaching its present dominant place in our culture.”

Author's Introduction

This is the pattern repeated across every example in the essay: not that the West alone had science, art, or government, but that only the West systematized these pursuits into specialized, trained, rule-governed institutions. Rationalization, in Weber's sense, is that specific move from skilled practice to organized, replicable, expert system. Hold onto this sentence. Weber will use exactly this concept of rationalization again on bureaucracy in Week 11.

3. What capitalism actually is. After naming and dismissing simple greed as the explanation, Weber gives his own definition:

“…capitalism is identical with the pursuit of profit, and forever renewed profit, by means of continuous, rational, capitalistic enterprise.”

Author's Introduction

Read this against the sentence just before it, where Weber lists waiters, physicians, soldiers, and beggars as people who have always wanted money. His point is that wanting money is not capitalism, and capitalism can even require restraining that impulse rather than indulging it. What makes an enterprise capitalistic, on this definition, is continuity and rational calculation, not appetite. This is the definition Ritzer leans on when you meet his ideas about rationalization later in the course, and it is worth being able to state precisely rather than approximately.

✏️ AFTER YOU READ

Write short answers to the four questions in your Sociological Journal document.

▶  Answer question 1 in your notebook first, then open this.
Saying the West was simply richer or more advanced assumes what needs to be explained. Weber's list shows that other civilizations had comparable or superior achievements in specific fields: Indian geometry, Chinese historical scholarship, Babylonian astronomy. If wealth or general advancement explained the difference, we would expect that advantage to show up everywhere at once, not in one specific and repeatable form (rational, systematic, specialized) that shows up only in the West. Explaining a pattern that specific requires a cause that specific, which is exactly why Weber will spend the rest of the book looking at something as narrow and unlikely as a set of religious doctrines.
▶  Decide in your notebook how this method differs from Durkheim's, then open this.
Durkheim measures a rate within a single kind of society and asks what varies it: religion, marital status, and so on, all inside roughly comparable European nations. Weber instead compares whole civilizations against one another across the entire span of world history, asking why a pattern appears in exactly one of them and nowhere else. Durkheim's method can tell you that a factor correlates with a rate with real statistical confidence, but it works within a single kind of case. Weber's method can range across radically different societies and time periods, but it cannot produce anything like a statistical correlation; it has to argue, case by case, that the comparison is a fair one. Neither method is simply better. Each can show something the other cannot.

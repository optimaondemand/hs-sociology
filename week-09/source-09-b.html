PRIMARY SOURCE 9B
Rational, Traditional, and Charismatic Authority
Weber, Economy and Society, Part One, Chapter III, Sections 1–4: The Types of Legitimate Domination.
💡 WHERE THIS FITS
This is the chapter Lesson 9.03 was built from: Weber's own, more careful statement of the three types, including the warning that they are ideal types, built in their purest form on purpose, and no real historical case is a clean example of just one.
📖 WHY YOU ARE READING THIS

This is one of the most widely cited passages in all of sociological theory. Weber is making a purely analytical distinction. His three types are ideal types, not empirical descriptions of any particular government, so read them as analytical tools rather than as descriptions of history.

The chapter moves in seven steps. Track them and the argument holds together much better than it does on a first skim.

HOW THE CHAPTER IS BUILT
1
Define domination generally. The probability that specific commands will find obedience among a given group, resting on any motive from dull habit to rational calculation.
2
Name the three pure bases of legitimacy. Rational, traditional, and charismatic, stated as a claim each system makes for itself.
3
Say what is actually obeyed in each case. An impersonal order in the rational case, a person bound by custom in the traditional case, a person's proven gift in the charismatic case.
4
Enter the caveat. None of the three appears in a historically pure form. The types are analytical, not descriptive, and the routinization of charisma will later bring the analysis closer to real cases.
5
Define charisma precisely. An extraordinary quality attributed to a person by those who follow them, whatever an outside observer might think of it "objectively."
6
Set charisma against the other two. Bureaucratic domination is rational, bound by analysable rules. Traditional domination is bound by precedent. Charismatic domination is free of rules and overturns the past; it is revolutionary in a way the other two cannot be.
7
Show charisma's strained relation to economic life. Pure charisma rejects ordinary, regular economic activity as a source of income, even when it still seeks material power by other means.
🎯 READ FOR THESE FOUR QUESTIONS
  1. How does Weber define domination, Herrschaft? Is it different from what we have been calling authority? If so, how?
  2. Weber says charismatic authority is specifically foreign to economic considerations. What does he mean? Why would charismatic authority be in tension with economic rationality?
  3. Give one contemporary example of each of Weber's three types of authority. Do any of these examples suggest that the types are not mutually exclusive? Can an authority claim legitimacy on multiple grounds simultaneously?
  4. Weber argues that charismatic authority tends to be unstable. What historical examples can you identify of the routinization of charisma? Does the routinized institution ever preserve any of the original charismatic spirit?
💡 ABOUT THIS TRANSLATION
Weber wrote in German. This English rendering was prepared for this course from the German original. Three words are worth flagging before you meet them. Herrschaft is translated as domination, and it is broader and more neutral in German than the English word suggests. It names any relationship in which commands are reliably obeyed, not only oppressive ones. Beruf, translated as calling, and Sendung, translated as mission, both carry a vocational, almost religious weight in German that a single English word only partly captures.
WHAT DOMINATION MEANS

Domination shall mean, by definition (Chapter I, §16), the probability of finding obedience to specific (or all) commands among a given group of people. It does not mean every kind of probability of exercising power or influence over other people. Domination, or authority, in this sense may, in individual cases, rest on the most varied motives for compliance, ranging from dull habit to purely rational calculations of advantage. A certain minimum willingness to obey—that is, an interest, external or internal, in obeying—belongs to every genuine relationship of domination.

A discussion of how domination is distinct from a merely contractual claim, such as a worker's claim to his wage, and of how the legitimacy of a system of domination is a matter of the probability that it will be regarded and treated as legitimate in practice, whatever the actual motives for individual obedience may be, is omitted here.
THE THREE PURE TYPES

There are three pure types of legitimate domination. Its claim to legitimacy may rest primarily on a rational basis, resting on belief in the legality of enacted rules and the right of those appointed under them to exercise authority, legal domination; or a traditional basis, resting on the everyday belief in the sanctity of traditions that have always existed and in the legitimacy of those called by those traditions to exercise authority, traditional domination; or finally a charismatic basis, resting on extraordinary devotion to the sanctity, heroism, or exemplary character of a person, and to the orders revealed or created by that person, charismatic domination.

HOW OBEDIENCE IS RENDERED

In the case of legally established domination, obedience is rendered to the legally enacted, impersonal order and to the superior designated by it, by virtue of the formal legality of his commands and within the sphere of their authority. In the case of traditional domination, obedience is rendered to the person of the ruler who is called by tradition and bound to tradition, within its sphere, by virtue of piety and within the limits of what is customary. In the case of charismatic domination, obedience is rendered to the leader qualified by charisma as such, by virtue of personal trust in his revelation, heroism, or exemplary character, and within the sphere of validity of the belief in that charisma.

TYPES, NOT HISTORIES

The fact that none of the three ideal types discussed below ordinarily appears in a historically pure form should no more prevent us here than elsewhere from defining them in their purest possible form. Later the modification of pure charisma through its routinization will be discussed, thereby bringing the analysis much closer to empirical forms of domination. But even then, every empirical historical instance of domination remains something that is not, as the saying goes, a cleverly contrived book. Sociological typology merely offers empirical historical research the advantage, often not to be underestimated, that in any particular case it can indicate what is charismatic, hereditary charismatic, official charismatic, patriarchal, bureaucratic, estate-based, and so forth, or what approximates these types, while working with reasonably unambiguous concepts. It is as far as possible from the intention here to suggest that the whole of historical reality can be captured within the conceptual scheme developed below.

The detailed sections defining legal domination with a bureaucratic administrative staff, and traditional domination in its patriarchal, patrimonial, and estate-based variants, are omitted here.
WHAT CHARISMA IS

Charisma shall mean a quality of an individual personality that is regarded as extraordinary, originally, whether in prophets, healers, legal sages, hunting leaders, or war heroes, as resulting from magical powers, by virtue of which that person is considered endowed with supernatural or superhuman powers or qualities, or at least with specifically exceptional powers or qualities not accessible to everyone else, or is regarded as sent by God or as exemplary and therefore valued as a leader. From the standpoint of sociology, it is entirely irrelevant how this quality might be judged objectively from any ethical, aesthetic, or other point of view. What matters alone is how it is actually evaluated by those subject to charismatic domination, the followers.

A discussion of how charismatic authority depends on continued proof and recognition, and of the emotional, non-bureaucratic character of the charismatic administrative staff, no appointment, no salaries, no permanent offices, only calling by the leader, is omitted here.
CHARISMA AGAINST RULE AND TRADITION

Charismatic domination, as the extraordinary, stands in sharp opposition both to rational domination, especially bureaucratic domination, and to traditional domination, especially patriarchal, patrimonial, and estate domination. The latter are specific forms of everyday domination; genuine charismatic domination is specifically their opposite. Bureaucratic domination is specifically rational, in the sense that it is bound by rules capable of discursive analysis. Charismatic domination is specifically irrational, in the sense that it is free from rules. Traditional domination is bound to the precedents of the past and is therefore likewise oriented toward rules. Charismatic domination overturns the past, within its sphere, and is in this sense specifically revolutionary.

CHARISMA AND ECONOMIC LIFE

Pure charisma is specifically foreign to economic life. Wherever it appears, it constitutes a calling in the emphatic sense of the word, as a mission or an inner task. In its pure type, it despises and rejects the economic exploitation of charismatic gifts as a source of income, though this often remains more an ideal than a reality. This is not to say that charisma always renounces property and acquisition, as prophets and their disciples sometimes do. The war hero and his followers seek booty; the plebiscitary ruler or charismatic party leader seeks the material means of power; and the former also seeks the material splendor of rule in order to strengthen his prestige as ruler. What all of them reject, so long as the genuinely charismatic type remains intact, is traditional or rational everyday economic activity, that is, the obtaining of regular income through continuous economic activity directed toward that end. Support through patronage, especially lavish patronage such as gifts, endowments, bribes, and generous gratuities, or even through begging, on the one hand, and booty, violent extortion, or formally peaceful exaction, on the other, are the typical forms by which charisma provides for its material needs. From the standpoint of a rational economy, charisma is a typical force of economic irrationality. For it rejects every integration into ordinary economic life. At most, with complete inner indifference, it may simply take advantage of occasional and irregular opportunities for gain.

Examples of this principle, drawn from several historical religious communities and how they handled their material needs, are omitted here.

Charisma is the great revolutionary force in epochs bound by tradition. Unlike the equally revolutionary force of reason, which operates either directly from without, by changing the conditions and problems of life and thereby indirectly changing people's orientations toward them, or through intellectualization, charisma can bring about a transformation from within. Born of distress or enthusiasm, it can produce a transformation in the central direction of attitudes and action, involving a complete reorientation of every attitude toward every individual sphere of life and toward the world as a whole. In pre-rational epochs, tradition and charisma together account for almost the entire range of orientations of human action.

✏️ AFTER YOU READ

Write short answers to the four questions. For question 3, use real examples, not the ones in the lesson.

▶  Answer question 1 in your notebook first, then open this.
Weber's domination is broader and colder than the ordinary sense of authority. He defines it purely by the probability that a command will actually be obeyed, whatever the motive behind the obedience happens to be, from dull habit to naked calculation. Authority, as this course has been using the word, already carries a normative weight: it implies the obedience is owed, not merely observed. Weber's definition deliberately brackets that question at first, so that domination can be studied as a fact before legitimacy is introduced as a separate, further claim resting on top of it. That is a methodological choice, not an accident, and it is the same move Durkheim made in Week 3 when he insisted on studying social facts as things before asking what they mean.
▶  Decide in your notebook whether Weber's caveat about pure types is a strength of the theory or an admission of weakness. Then open this.
There is a real objection here worth taking seriously rather than waving off. A typology that admits no real case is a pure instance of any of its categories risks explaining everything and therefore nothing, since any messy mixture can always be described as "some blend of the three." Weber's own defense, in the passage on types and histories above, is that the value of an ideal type is not descriptive accuracy but analytical leverage: it lets a researcher say precisely which elements of a real, mixed case are doing which kind of legitimating work, using concepts unambiguous enough to compare across very different societies. Whether that defense fully answers the objection is a fair question to leave open. A weaker but honest position is that the typology is most useful exactly where you used it this week, on the hard, blended cases, and least useful as a label to stick on a case and stop thinking.

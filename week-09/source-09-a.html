PRIMARY SOURCE 9A
A Second Reading
There is no new text this week. You choose one primary source from Weeks 1–8 and read it again, closely, with everything the quarter has since taught you.
📖 WHY YOU ARE DOING THIS

You read Aristotle in Week 1 knowing almost no sociology. You would read him differently now, having spent eight weeks acquiring the vocabulary and the habits of attention that sociology teaches. The same is true of every other source you have met this quarter. A text does not change between a first reading and a second one. The reader does, and a text that seemed simple, or difficult for reasons you could not quite name, often reveals itself very differently once you know more.

This is also direct preparation for two things ahead of you. The Classical Thinker Profile, due at the end of Week 8, already asked you to engage one primary source closely; this exercise sharpens that same skill. And the Community Institution Study, launching this week, will ask you to look at something you have already seen (a real institution near you) with a sharper eye than your first glance gave it. Re-reading a text you already know is the same discipline, applied to a page instead of a place.

HOW TO CHOOSE
1
Pick the one you argued with, not the one you liked. A source you agreed with easily probably has less left to teach you on a second pass. A source that frustrated you, that you resisted, or that you are still not sure you accept, is far more likely to reward a closer look.
2
Pick the one you understood least the first time. If a guide question stumped you in the moment, that is now a lead, not a failure. You have eight more weeks of vocabulary than you had then.
3
Pick the one most useful to you later. If you already suspect which thinker you will lean on for the Community Institution Study, or if a text connects to the setting you are considering for it, re-reading it now is not wasted time.

🔍 A DIFFERENT SET OF QUESTIONS THIS TIME

Do not simply reread the guide questions you already answered. Read the passage itself again, and use these four instead. They ask something a first reading cannot answer.

1
What did you miss? What did you miss or misunderstand on your first reading that is clearer now? Be specific.
2
What does it connect to? How does this thinker's argument connect to at least two other thinkers from the quarter? What would they say to each other?
3
What is still unanswered? What question does this thinker raise that the course has not yet fully answered?
4
What is the one sentence? Write one sentence that represents, in your own words, the single most important idea from this thinker. Not a summary: a claim you are willing to defend.
✏️ AFTER YOU READ

Write short answers to the four questions in your Sociological Journal document. Your fourth answer, the single defensible sentence, is excellent raw material for Thursday's journal entry, which asks you to argue for a position using at least three thinkers from the quarter. Do not throw it away.

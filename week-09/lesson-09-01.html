Notice that almost nobody in this arc is disagreeing about facts. They are disagreeing about what the facts mean, which is exactly the distinction Wednesday's lesson takes up directly.

Quarter 1 has covered nine weeks and more than ten thinkers: Aristotle, Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau, Tocqueville, Durkheim, Simmel, Tonnies, Nisbet, and Putnam, alongside Berger, whose ideas you met even though the assigned reading in his place came from Simmel. It can appear, surveying this list, that they are simply a gallery of thinkers who share only the broad label of "sociology." A closer look reveals that they are all addressing a single family of questions. What is the relationship between the individual and the society that formed her? How do social bonds originate, and what are they made of? What happens to individuals and communities when those bonds weaken? And what can sociology, as a discipline, contribute to understanding these questions?

These are not merely academic questions. They are the questions that arise naturally for anyone living in a modern society and paying attention. Why do I feel the way I feel about certain obligations? Why do some communities hold together and others dissolve? Why do some forms of social change feel like liberation and others feel like loss? Sociology does not answer these questions definitively, but it provides the vocabulary, the analytical tools, and the empirical evidence to engage them more honestly and more precisely than common sense alone allows.

📚 PART 2 · KEY TERMS FOR REVIEW
Social Fact
Durkheim: external, general, constraining. The basic object of sociological analysis.
Socialization
The process by which individuals internalize social norms, values, and roles. Primary and secondary.
Mechanical / Organic Solidarity
Durkheim's two types: sameness versus interdependence.
Anomie
The condition of normlessness in modern societies when the collective conscience weakens.
Gemeinschaft / Gesellschaft
Tonnies: community based on personal bonds versus association based on contract.
Social Capital
Putnam: networks of trust and reciprocity. Bonding versus bridging.
Intermediate Institution
Nisbet: the voluntary associations between individual and state that human freedom requires.
Sociological Consciousness
Berger: the capacity to see through social appearances to underlying structures.

You have already read a full definition of each of these. The activity below tests something different: not whether you can define a term, but whether you can remember which thinker it belongs to without the term sitting right next to the name.

📚 PART 3 · THE ARC OF THE ARGUMENT

The course began with two disruptions: the French Revolution and the Industrial Revolution. These events did not merely change political arrangements or economic production. They dissolved the social bonds, the community structures, and the shared meanings that had organized European life for centuries. Sociology was born as the attempt to understand what had been lost, what had been gained, and what new forms of social life might be possible.

Aristotle provided the foundational claim that humans are social animals who cannot fully flourish outside political community. Hobbes challenged it, arguing that without external constraint, social life is war. Locke and Rousseau offered alternatives: rational contract and natural goodness, respectively. These pre-sociological debates established the terms of argument that sociology would inherit.

Durkheim then showed, with empirical rigor, that society is a reality, not merely an agreement among individuals but a structure with its own forces (social facts) and its own forms of solidarity. Simmel explored the texture of modern social life: the blase attitude, the money economy, the stranger. Berger's approach, which you met through the reading substitute, taught the same lesson in a different key: to see sociologically is to look through appearances to the structures beneath. Tonnies named what was being lost, Gemeinschaft giving way to Gesellschaft. Nisbet and Putnam documented, across a century, the costs of that loss for democracy, human flourishing, and individual meaning.

⚡ TRY IT · WHICH CONCEPT IS THIS?
Each scenario is new. Decide which Quarter 1 concept it illustrates, and watch for the near-neighbor that would also seem plausible.
1. A neighborhood association brings together people of different ethnic backgrounds, income levels, and religions to run a shared community garden. Members say the garden connects them to people they would otherwise never meet.
2. A small farming village where every household grows the same crops, worships the same way, and would react identically to an outsider's crime.
3. A large modern city where residents interact mainly through contracts, employment, and paid transactions, and where a person's role matters more than any personal bond with the people performing it.
4. A student learns that a company's stated reason for a policy is customer service, but the real driver is reducing legal liability, and starts asking what other institutions might be doing beneath their stated purpose.
📚 PART 4 · WHAT QUARTER 2 WILL BUILD

Quarter 2 turns from the foundational questions of sociological theory to the specific institutions through which societies organize power, production, and community: government, law, bureaucracy, the economy, and the cultural institutions of modern life. The thinkers of Quarter 1 will return as interlocutors. Weber will join on bureaucracy and authority, Marx on capitalism and class, Durkheim again on crime and deviance, Tocqueville again on American institutions. The question of how individuals become social beings, which has occupied Quarter 1, gives way to the question of how societies organize themselves.

▶  Predict in your notebook one Quarter 1 thinker whose ideas you expect to see argued with, not just repeated, in Quarter 2. Then open this.
A strong candidate is Durkheim. He returns in Weeks 12 and 13 on the division of labor and on crime and deviance, but Marx enters Quarter 2 as a direct rival, arguing that the divisions Durkheim treats as functional interdependence are, in fact, a structure of exploitation. Watch for the pattern: Quarter 2 rarely introduces a thinker in isolation. It sets one against another and asks you to judge the contest, which is a harder and more honest kind of learning than adding names to a list.

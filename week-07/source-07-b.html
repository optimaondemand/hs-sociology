THE BOOK

START AT
Book One, Section 1, "Embryo or Emergent Forms." It opens: “In accordance with the preliminary explanations, the theory of Gemeinschaft starts from the assumption of perfect unity of human wills…” This section grounds Gemeinschaft in the body itself: birth, parental descent, and sex, working through the three primal relationships of mother and child, husband and wife, and brothers and sisters.
CONTINUE THROUGH
Book One, Sections 2 through 5, which extend the argument through neighborhood, friendship, and the customs and religious life that bind a Gemeinschaft together. Read this stretch in full, using your edition's section headings as your guide.
THEN READ
Book Two, Section 1, "The Forms of Human Will." It opens: “The concept of human will, the correct interpretation of which is essential to the subject of this treatise, implies a twofold meaning…” This is where natural will (Wesenwille) and rational will (Kürwille) are defined and tied back to Gemeinschaft and Gesellschaft.

💡 WHERE THIS FITS
Wednesday's lesson gave you Gemeinschaft and Gesellschaft as a finished pair, and paired each with a Durkheim and a Tocqueville equivalent. This reading is where Tönnies actually builds Gemeinschaft, and he does not start from the village or the guild the way the lesson's examples did. He starts from something more primal: the body itself, birth, and the bond between mother and child, before working outward to neighborhood and friendship, and only then over to Book Two's psychology of the two wills. Watch the direction of the argument; it runs from biology to psychology to sociology, not the reverse.
📖 WHY YOU ARE READING THIS

You already met a structurally similar distinction in Durkheim's mechanical and organic solidarity in Week 4. Read here for the difference in tone, not only the similarity in shape. Durkheim treats the shift from one form of cohesion to the other as basically a successful transformation, purchased at the price of a manageable pathology he calls anomie. Tönnies is far less willing to call the shift from Gemeinschaft to Gesellschaft a successful trade. Watch for whether the text gives him good grounds for that harsher verdict, or whether it is closer to a preference dressed as an argument.

The reading moves in five steps. The gap noted above falls between steps two and three.

1
Ground Gemeinschaft in the body. Birth, parental descent, and sex bind human wills together before either party has made any choice at all.
2
Rank the three primal relationships. Mother and child, husband and wife, and siblings, each binding differently and each rooted more in instinct and habituation than in agreement.
⋯
Sections 2 through 5, not anchored here. Neighborhood, friendship, and the customs and religion that extend Gemeinschaft beyond kinship. Read this stretch in full using your own edition.
3
Pivot to psychology. Define two kinds of will, natural and rational, as two different relationships between will and thinking.
4
State the correspondence. Natural will underlies Gemeinschaft; rational will underlies Gesellschaft. Tönnies insists this pairing is not a late addition to his theory but follows from the whole line of reasoning.
🎯 READ FOR THESE FOUR QUESTIONS
  1. How does Tönnies distinguish "natural will" (Wesenwille) from "rational will" (Kürwille)? Give one example of each type of will operating in a social relationship.
  2. Tönnies argues that the family is the paradigm case of Gemeinschaft. What features of family life make it the clearest example of community in his sense?
  3. Is Tönnies's account of Gesellschaft purely negative, a description of loss, or does he see genuine goods in the contractual, impersonal forms of modern society? Support your answer with textual evidence.
  4. Durkheim would say that the shift from mechanical to organic solidarity is not a loss but a transformation. Is this a convincing reply to Tönnies? What would Tönnies say in response?
🔍 THREE SENTENCES TO FIND AND MARK

Three sentences anchor this reading. Find each one in your copy and mark it, then read the note underneath.

1. The definition. Book Two opens by naming the two wills directly:

“The will of the human being in the first form I call natural will (Wesenwille); in the second form, rational will (Kürwille).”

Book Two, Section 1

Wesenwille draws on Wesen, meaning being or essence, so the term suggests a will continuous with what a person organically is. Kürwille draws on a root meaning to choose or select, suggesting a will that operates by picking among alternatives toward a separately conceived end. Notice Tönnies does not call the second will "rational" because it is smarter. He calls it rational because it calculates, and calculation is exactly what the first will does not do.

2. The paradigm case. Before he reaches any theory of will at all, Tönnies grounds Gemeinschaft in the plainest relationship he can find:

“The relation between mother and child is most deeply rooted in liking or in pure instinct.”

Book One, Section 1

This is deliberately unsentimental. Tönnies is not describing chosen, romantic love; he is describing something closer to biology, continuous with the "coherence of vegetative life" he mentions in the surrounding text. That is his strongest possible case for Gemeinschaft, because nothing about it was agreed to by either party. Test guide question 2 against this sentence: is family the paradigm case of community because it is the strongest bond, or because it is the least chosen one?

3. The parallel structure. Late in Book Two, Section 1, Tönnies pairs the two wills with two matched lists of terms:

“Special emphasis must be laid on the fact that the forms of will such as liking, habit, and memory are as essential for and characteristic of Gemeinschaft-like associations as those of deliberation, decision, and concept are of Gesellschaft-like associations.”

Book Two, Section 1

Read the two lists against each other. Liking, habit, and memory are all things that happen to a person; deliberation, decision, and concept are all things a person does. The sentence's structure is doing real argumentative work: it is claiming that an entire way of being related to other people, passive versus active, receiving versus choosing, tracks the Gemeinschaft/Gesellschaft line exactly. This is the same distinction Durkheim's mechanical and organic solidarity draws, but pinned to individual psychology rather than to the structure of a whole society. Where Durkheim asks what binds a society together, Tönnies asks what kind of will a person is exercising inside each kind of bond.

✏️ AFTER YOU READ

Write short answers to the four questions in your Sociological Journal document. Question 2 will feed directly into tonight's journal entry, so answer it with a real example from your own life.

▶  Answer question 3 in your notebook first, then open this.
Sections 2 through 5, which you read directly from the book, are where the strongest evidence for either side of this question will actually live, so treat what follows as a starting frame rather than the full answer. From what the lesson and the anchored passages above establish, Tönnies is at minimum honest that Gesellschaft brings real goods: individuals gain freedom from inherited obligation, the ability to choose associations, occupations, and beliefs that Gemeinschaft's members never could. That is not nothing, and a fair reading credits him with it. But notice how he frames those goods: as compensations for a loss, not as an independent achievement worth wanting for its own sake. Whether your own reading of Sections 2 through 5 finds him being more generous to Gesellschaft than that, or less, is exactly the kind of textual evidence question 3 is asking you to settle for yourself.
▶  Decide in your notebook how Tönnies would answer question 4. Then open this.
Tönnies's likely reply is that calling the shift a "transformation" rather than a "loss" is exactly the kind of redescription that hides what actually happened. On his account, natural will and rational will are not two settings on the same dial that a society can slide between without cost; they are different in kind, rooted in a different relationship between will and thought. A society that trades one for the other has not converted community into a new, equally valid form of community. It has replaced community with something that resembles it only from a distance, held together by contract rather than by shared life. Durkheim's confidence that organic solidarity is a genuine, workable form of cohesion is, from this angle, close to changing the subject: it answers "what still holds the society together," which is a real question, but not "what was lost," which is Tönnies's question. Whether that counts as Tönnies winning the exchange, or merely as the two of them asking different questions past each other, is worth deciding for yourself, and it is exactly the kind of judgment call the Community Institution Study will ask you to make about a real institution in Weeks 9 through 11.

💡 WHERE THIS FITS
Monday's lesson described a hidden curriculum operating whether or not anyone designed it. This chapter is Durkheim's own argument for why that should not surprise you: he treats the entire educational system of a society, its stated content and its unstated pressures alike, as a social fact in the strict Week 3 sense. Reread the three-gate test before you start. You will use it again here.
📖 WHY YOU ARE READING THIS

Durkheim opens by separating two words most people use interchangeably: education, the practice, and pedagogy, the theory of the practice. That distinction is not pedantry. It is the hinge his whole argument turns on, because it lets him treat education as an object a science can study, in the same move he made for social facts generally in Week 3.

The excerpt moves in six steps. Track them, because the supplied text has gaps between some of them, marked below, and knowing the shape of the argument helps you bridge each gap without inventing what is missing.

HOW THE EXCERPT IS BUILT
1
Separate education from pedagogy. Education is action, continuous and mostly unconscious. Pedagogy is theory about that action, and the two can even conflict.
2
Apply the social-fact test. A society's educational system is external to any individual and coercive, resisted at the cost of the same kind of pushback Week 3 described.
3
Claim one science, not many. However different educational practices look across societies, all of them do the same job: adapting one generation to the social environment it must live in.
4
Survey the forms across history. Diffuse tribal education, priestly education under a religious caste, and education divided between family and a secular state in Greece and Rome.
5
Add the individual back in. Once a culture values individual personality, education must also cultivate the child's particular temperament, not only impose a uniform pattern.
6
Conclude with pedagogy's purpose. Reflection on method is what keeps education from hardening into rigid, oppressive routine. Routine, not tradition itself, is what he warns against.
🎯 READ FOR THESE FOUR QUESTIONS
  1. How does Durkheim define education? What does his definition reveal about his view of the relationship between society and the individual?
  2. Durkheim says that the "ideal of education" varies from society to society and from period to period. What does this imply about the claim that there is a universal standard of good education?
  3. Does Durkheim's account of education as social transmission leave any room for individual critical thinking and moral development, or does it reduce education entirely to social control?
  4. Aristotle argued in the Politics that education is the responsibility of the state because citizens are the raw material of the polis. How does this compare to Durkheim's account? Where do they agree and disagree?

Durkheim wrote in French. This English rendering was prepared for this course from that public-domain original and carries no modern translator's credit, the same house practice used for the Week 3 Durkheim excerpt. One word is worth flagging before you meet it: pédagogie in French, rendered here as pedagogy, means the theory of educational method, not classroom technique in the narrower modern sense.
EDUCATION VERSUS PEDAGOGY

The words education and pedagogy have often been confused, although they ought to be carefully distinguished.

Education is the action exercised upon children by parents and teachers. This action is continual and universal. There is no period of social life, indeed, scarcely a moment in the day, in which the younger generations are not in contact with their elders and, consequently, receiving from them an educational influence. For this influence is not confined to the brief moments when parents or teachers consciously communicate, by means of formal instruction, the results of their experience to those who come after them. There is an unconscious education that never ceases. By our example, by the words we speak, and by the actions we perform, we continually shape the soul of our children.

Pedagogy is quite different. It consists not in actions but in theories. These theories are ways of conceiving education, not ways of practicing it. At times they differ so greatly from the educational practices in use that they even stand in opposition to them. The pedagogical theories of Rabelais, Rousseau, and Pestalozzi were all at odds with the education of their own age. Education, therefore, is merely the subject matter of pedagogy. Pedagogy consists in a particular way of reflecting upon educational matters.

EDUCATION AS A SOCIAL FACT

For the educational system in use within a given society, considered at a given stage of its development, consists of a body of practices, ways of acting, and customs that constitute perfectly definite facts possessing the same reality as other social facts. They are not, as was long believed, more or less arbitrary and artificial combinations owing their existence solely to the capricious influence of contingent individual wills. On the contrary, they constitute genuine social institutions. No individual can make it the case that a society possesses, at a given moment, an educational system other than that implied by its social structure, just as it is impossible for a living organism to possess organs and functions other than those implied by its constitution.

If, in addition to all the reasons already advanced in support of this conception, further reasons are required, it is enough to become aware of the compelling force with which these educational practices impose themselves upon us. It is vain to imagine that we raise our children however we please. We are obliged to follow the rules prevailing in the social environment in which we live. Public opinion imposes them upon us, and public opinion is a moral force whose coercive power is no less than that of physical forces. Customs invested with its authority are thereby withdrawn, to a large extent, from the control of individuals. We may indeed violate them, but in doing so the moral forces against which we rebel react against us, and, because of their superiority, it is difficult for us not to be overcome.

ONE SCIENCE OF EDUCATION

Moreover, whatever their particular form, and however great the differences among them, all educational practices share one essential characteristic: they all result from the action exercised by one generation upon the next with a view to adapting the latter to the social environment in which it is destined to live. They are therefore all different manifestations of this fundamental relation. Consequently, they are facts of the same kind, belonging to the same logical category, and may therefore constitute the object of one and the same science, the science of education.

DIFFUSE EDUCATION UNDER THE TRIBAL SYSTEM

For example, under the tribal system, the essential characteristic of education is that it is diffuse. It is imparted indiscriminately by all members of the clan. There are no designated teachers, no special supervisors entrusted with the formation of the young; rather, all the elders, the entire body of preceding generations, perform this role. At most, in the case of certain particularly important forms of instruction, specific elders may be more especially designated.

In other, more advanced societies, this diffusion comes to an end, or at least becomes less pronounced. Education is concentrated in the hands of specialized officials. In India and Egypt, for example, it is the priests who are charged with this function. Education becomes an attribute of priestly authority.

EDUCATION DIVIDED BETWEEN STATE AND FAMILY

In other societies, such as the Greek and Latin city-states, education remains divided, though in varying proportions from one city to another, between the state and the family. There is no priestly caste. The state itself is entrusted with religious life. Consequently, because the state has no speculative needs and is oriented above all toward action and practical affairs, science arises outside the state and therefore also outside religion whenever the need for it is felt. The philosophers and scientists of Greece are private individuals and laymen. Science itself quickly develops an anti-religious tendency. From the standpoint that concerns us here, the result is that instruction likewise assumes, from the moment of its appearance, a secular and private character. The grammateus of Athens is simply a private citizen, possessing neither official status nor religious office.

WHERE THE INDIVIDUAL ENTERS

The educational ideal of any age expresses, above all, the condition of the society to which it belongs. Yet for that ideal to become a reality, it must be embodied in the consciousness of the child. The human mind, however, has laws of its own, and these laws must be understood if one wishes to modify it while avoiding, so far as possible, the empirical trial and error that pedagogy exists precisely to reduce to a minimum.

Indeed, once individual personality has become an essential element in the intellectual and moral culture of humanity, the educator must take account of the germ of individuality present in every child. By every possible means, he must seek to foster its development. Instead of applying the same impersonal and uniform discipline to everyone without exception, he must vary and diversify his methods according to each child's temperament and the particular cast of his mind. But to adapt educational practices intelligently to this diversity of individual cases, one must understand the ends toward which those practices are directed, the reasons underlying the various methods that compose them, and the effects they produce under different circumstances. In a word, they must first have been subjected to pedagogical reflection.

An empirical, mechanical education cannot fail to become repressive and leveling.

REFLECTION AGAINST ROUTINE

Moreover, as history advances, the pace of social evolution becomes more rapid. One age no longer resembles the one that preceded it; each period has its own distinctive character. New needs and new ideas continually arise. If education is to respond to these constant changes in opinion and in manners, it too must change and therefore remain sufficiently flexible to permit change. The only way to prevent it from falling under the tyranny of habit and degenerating into rigid, mechanical automatism is to keep it continually alive through reflection.

When educators understand the methods they employ, together with their purpose and rationale, they are able to judge them and therefore remain prepared to modify them whenever they become convinced that the end to be pursued is no longer the same or that different means are required. Reflection is, above all else, the force opposed to routine, and routine is the chief obstacle to necessary progress.

✏️ AFTER YOU READ

Write short answers to the four questions in your Sociological Journal document.

▶  Answer question 2 in your notebook first, then open this.
If the educational ideal genuinely varies with the society it serves, tribal, priestly, or divided between state and family, then there is no single fixed standard of "good education" sitting outside history, waiting to be discovered. What counts as good education is relative to what a given social environment actually needs its next generation to become. This does not mean Durkheim thinks all educational systems are equally defensible; a system can still be judged by whether it succeeds at adapting children to the environment they will actually live in. It does mean he would reject any claim that a single ideal, ancient Athens's or Prussia's or your own school's, is simply correct for every society at every time. That is a genuinely uncomfortable conclusion for anyone who wants education to rest on timeless principles, and it is worth deciding for yourself whether the discomfort is a flaw in his argument or a discovery about the subject.
▶  Decide in your notebook whether question 3's charge of "social control" sticks. Then open this.
Durkheim's own text gives you material for both sides. The social-control reading is strongest in the passage on public opinion's coercive power and the reminder that we do not raise children however we please. But the passage on individual personality complicates that reading considerably: Durkheim explicitly says the educator must cultivate "the germ of individuality present in every child" rather than apply "the same impersonal and uniform discipline to everyone." An education aimed only at social control would have no reason to care about that germ at all. The honest answer is probably that Durkheim wants both, transmission of what society genuinely needs and cultivation of what makes each child distinct, and does not think the two are in conflict as often as a critic might assume. Whether he is right that they usually cohere, rather than usually compete, is a fair question to carry into Monday's lesson on the hidden curriculum, which is precisely a case where the two goals can pull apart.

APPENDIX D
Sociological Journal Guide
One journal entry every week, all eighteen weeks. Read this once, in Week 1, and the pattern will make sense for the rest of the course.
📖 WHAT THE JOURNAL IS FOR

The Sociological Journal trains a specific habit: applying a theoretical framework to something you actually observed, and moving between the personal and the structural, the specific and the general. It is not a diary, and it is not a summary of the week's lesson. It is where you practice sociological thinking about real social life, week after week, until it stops feeling like an assignment and starts feeling like a way of noticing things.

By Week 18 you will have eighteen entries. Read together, they are a record of how your sociological vision developed across the course, and that record is worth more than any single week's grade.

THE JOURNAL IS NOT THE SAME ASSIGNMENT EVERY WEEK
Check which kind of week it is before you look for a discussion board that is not there.
ODD WEEKS (1, 3, 5, 7...)
A Canvas Discussion. Your entry is visible to classmates, and you must reply to one classmate's entry by the following Thursday. This is a graded discussion, not just a posting task.
EVEN WEEKS (2, 4, 6, 8...)
A Canvas Assignment, scored on completion. You submit your entry directly; there is no peer reply this week, and none is expected.

Every week's Module Map states which kind that week is, so you never have to guess. The word count and the quality of thinking expected are the same either way; only the discussion and peer-reply requirement changes.

✏️ HOW TO WRITE A STRONG ENTRY
  1. Name a concept. Identify one or more specific concepts from the week's lesson, along with the thinker who developed it. Do not write about "sociology" in general; write about a specific concept.
  2. Identify a specific situation. Choose something specific from your own observation or experience: not "social media in general" but one platform, one interaction, one moment. The more specific the example, the stronger the entry.
  3. Apply the concept. Show what the concept reveals about the situation that you would not see without it. Name the specific feature it explains.
  4. Complicate it. Say what the concept does not explain, or where it falls short. Acknowledging a framework's limits is more honest, and more useful, than applying it as though it were obviously correct.
  5. Conclude. State what you learned. Has your understanding of the situation, the concept, or your own social experience changed?
📋 LENGTH AND FORMAT

Entries run 200 to 350 words, except on any week whose prompt states otherwise. Submit directly to the Canvas Discussion or Assignment for that week, not as a separate Word document. Write in full sentences and paragraphs, not bullet points. Proofread before you submit; your entry is read by your facilitator every week, and by a classmate on odd weeks.

💬 PEER RESPONSE (ODD WEEKS ONLY)

On odd weeks, reply to one classmate's entry by the following Thursday. A strong reply does at least one of these:

  • Identifies a concept your classmate applied well, and explains why it works.
  • Offers an alternative or complementary framework that illuminates the same situation differently.
  • Raises a specific question that complicates the classmate's analysis.

A reply that consists only of agreement or a compliment does not earn full credit. On even weeks, there is nothing to reply to, since even-week entries are not posted as discussions.

✅ GRADING

Odd weeks (Discussion, three-point scale, plus one point for the peer reply):

PointsWhat earns it
3 (full credit)Submitted by the deadline, meets the word count, names and correctly applies at least one specific concept, and engages a specific situation with real analytical attention.
2 (partial credit)Submitted, but thin, vague, or not genuinely applying a concept from the course.
0Not submitted by the deadline, or off-topic.
+1 (peer reply)Earned when the reply meets the peer-response criteria above.

Even weeks (Assignment, completion-scored, no peer reply):

ResultWhat earns it
CompleteSubmitted by the deadline, meets the word count, and names and applies at least one specific concept to a specific situation. The bar for a completion score is genuine engagement, not polish; an entry that clearly did the thinking passes even if the prose is rougher than an odd-week discussion post.
IncompleteNot submitted by the deadline, well under the word count, or does not engage a specific concept at all.
💡 THE JOURNAL AS A COURSE-LONG PROJECT
Before you write your Week 18 entry, reread your Week 1 entry. Notice what has changed in how you analyze social life. Week 18 will ask you to reflect on that change directly. Across the semester the journal is not eighteen separate assignments; it is one document of your intellectual development, written eighteen weeks at a time.

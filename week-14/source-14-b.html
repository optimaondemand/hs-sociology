THE BOOK
Georg Simmel, “The Sociology of the Senses,” translated by Mark Ritter and David Frisby, in Simmel on Culture: Selected Writings, edited by David Frisby and Mike Featherstone (London: Sage Publications, 1997), pages 109 to 120.
START AT
The essay titled “The Sociology of the Senses”, page 109 of this edition. It is a complete, self-contained essay, not an excerpt from a longer chapter, so the essay's own title is where you begin.
STOP AT
The end of the same essay, page 120, immediately before the next essay in the collection begins.
HOW LONG
About eleven pages. Set aside thirty to forty minutes.
WHAT TO SKIP
Nothing. It is short, and each sense (sight, then hearing, then smell) gets a distinct, compact treatment. Do not skip ahead to a "conclusion"; Simmel simply stops when he has treated the senses he means to treat.

💡 WHERE THIS FITS
Wednesday's lesson discussed Sherry Turkle's argument that communications technology has changed human connection in ways we have not fully reckoned with. Turkle's own book stays out of this page because it is still in copyright, but the question she is asking is not new. A full century before the smartphone, Simmel was asking what property of a sense, sight especially, makes a contact between two people genuinely reciprocal, and what happens when that property is removed. Money, which you met in Week 4, was already one of history's most effective devices for stripping reciprocity out of a relationship. A screen may be doing something related, or something different. That is the question to carry into this reading.
📖 WHY YOU ARE READING THIS

Simmel treats the senses as sociological instruments, not just biological ones. Sight, he argues, does something no other sense can do: it makes a relationship automatically mutual. Hearing, smell, and touch do not share that property, and the differences matter enormously for what kind of social bond each one can support.

The essay moves sense by sense. Track the property Simmel assigns to each one, because the whole argument depends on comparing them.

1
Start with the eye. The mutual glance is, for Simmel, the most direct and purest interaction that exists, because it cannot be one-sided: to look is automatically to be looked at.
2
Name the reciprocity. This built-in mutuality is what makes visual contact different in kind from other forms of contact, not just different in degree.
3
Turn to the face. If the eye is the organ of reciprocity, the face is what makes a person's inner life legible to another at all. Watch what Simmel thinks is lost when a relationship has no face to read.
4
Contrast the ear. Hearing lacks the eye's built-in mutuality. You can be heard without hearing back, and you can hear without being heard. Ask what kind of social bond that asymmetry permits or forecloses.
5
Watch for smell. Simmel goes on to treat the sense of smell in the same comparative spirit, asking what it contributes that sight and hearing cannot. Notice which of the three senses is hardest to imagine a technology replacing.
🎯 READ FOR THESE FOUR QUESTIONS
  1. Simmel calls the mutual glance the most direct and purest interaction that exists, because seeing another person's eyes and being seen by them happen in the same act. What does he mean by calling this "purest," and what changes in a relationship when that built-in reciprocity is removed?
  2. He distinguishes the ear from the eye by the ear's lack of this reciprocity: hearing does not require being heard in return. Apply this distinction to a technology of your choice, a phone call, a video call, a text thread, a livestream. Does it preserve the eye's reciprocity, the ear's asymmetry, or something Simmel has no category for?
  3. Simmel treats the face as the essential object of one person reading another. Why might a species that also has language still need a face-reading capacity this developed? What is lost when a technology substitutes text, or even a voice with no visible face, for it?
  4. Compare this essay's account of unmediated sensory contact to Simmel's account of the money economy from Week 4, where relationships become calculable and impersonal. Is a screen-mediated relationship closer to a face-to-face relationship, or closer to a monetary exchange? Defend your answer with evidence from both readings.
🔍 TWO SENTENCES TO FIND AND MARK

Two phrases carry the comparison the whole essay is built on. Find each one in your copy and mark it, then read the note underneath.

1. The reciprocity of the eye. Simmel names what makes visual contact unlike any other kind:

“…the connection and interaction of individuals that lies in the act of individuals looking at one another…”

Simmel, “The Sociology of the Senses,” trans. Ritter and Frisby, p. 111

The phrase is doing more work than it looks like. Simmel is not merely saying people communicate with their eyes. He is saying the act itself is structurally different from speech or gesture, because it is impossible to look at someone without the possibility of being looked back at settling the relationship into a mutual one automatically, before either party decides anything.

2. The asymmetry of the ear. Having established what sight does, Simmel states plainly what hearing does not do:

“…the ear is distinguished from the eye by the absence of that reciprocity which the look produces between one eye and another.”

Simmel, “The Sociology of the Senses,” trans. Ritter and Frisby, p. 115

Sit with what this rules out. A voice can reach you without your voice reaching back; a broadcast, a recording, and (Simmel could not have known, but the case fits his own category) a video a stranger posts and never sees you watch, are all instances of hearing without the structural mutuality that seeing supplies. If Simmel is right that reciprocity is what makes visual contact "the purest" interaction, then a technology built entirely on the ear's asymmetry, or on a sight that is recorded rather than exchanged, is not a lesser version of face-to-face contact. It is a different kind of contact, with a missing property rather than a reduced amount of the same one.

✏️ AFTER YOU READ

Write short answers to the four questions in your Sociological Journal document. Question 2 will help directly with tonight's journal entry.

▶  Sort a video call, a text thread, and a livestream by Simmel's categories before you open this.
A video call is the closest of the three to the eye's reciprocity: both parties can see and be seen at once, even if a screen mediates it, which is probably why most people report it feels least alienating of the three. A text thread has neither the eye's reciprocity nor the ear's asymmetry in a pure form; it substitutes a written trace for both, which is part of why tone is so easy to misread in one. A livestream is close to pure asymmetry: the audience sees without being seen, which is structurally the ear's condition applied to sight. That last case is the interesting one, because it shows reciprocity is a property of the arrangement, not of the sense being used. Sight can be made one-sided too, once the technology allows it.
▶  Decide whether a screen-mediated relationship is more like a face or more like money, then open this.
There is a real case for both answers, and the strongest response holds them together rather than picking one. Toward money: a feed or a message thread reduces a person to what is "objectively perceivable," in the same phrase Simmel used of the money economy in Week 4, and it does this by removing the involuntary mutuality of the glance. Toward the face: unlike a coin, a screen can still carry a face, a voice, and real information about another person's inner state, which a banknote never could. The honest answer is probably that communications technology is not one thing sociologically. Some of what it carries behaves like money, matter-of-fact and calculable; some of it behaves like a face, however degraded. Turkle's worry, and the one worth carrying into tonight's journal, is that people may not always notice which one they are getting.

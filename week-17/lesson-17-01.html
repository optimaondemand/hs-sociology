📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
If Tocqueville named the danger in 1840, why did four more books need to be written about it?
Every thinker you meet today is retracing a path Tocqueville marked out first. That is not a criticism of them. It means the diagnosis has held up, decade after decade, under different conditions, which is the strongest kind of evidence a social theory can get.

2

📚 PART 1 · HABITS OF THE HEART

In 1985 the sociologist Robert Bellah and four colleagues published Habits of the Heart: Individualism and Commitment in American Life. The title, borrowed directly from Tocqueville's language for the mores that made American democracy workable, announced the book's argument: American individualism had developed in ways that threatened the very habits, of commitment, of civic engagement, of loyalty to communities larger than the self, that the original Tocquevillean analysis had identified as democracy's moral precondition. The book was a bestseller. It remains one of the most cited works in American sociology.

Bellah and his colleagues conducted extensive interviews with ordinary Americans about their values, their commitments, and their understanding of the good life. What they found was a troubling uniformity. Most Americans, asked to articulate their deepest commitments, fell back on one of two languages. The first was utilitarian individualism, the vocabulary of costs, benefits, and self-interest. The second was expressive individualism, the vocabulary of authenticity, self-realization, and feeling. Both are individualistic to the core, and neither has the conceptual resources to articulate a commitment to something larger than the self that is not reducible to self-interest or self-expression (Habits of the Heart, ch. 6).

📚 PART 2 · ONE DIAGNOSIS, RETRACED FOUR TIMES

THE QUESTION RUNNING THROUGH ALL FIVE
What stands between the individual and the state, and what happens when it thins out? This is the question your Capstone will ask about one real community, not a whole nation.

Berger and Neuhaus's phrase for the institutions in the middle, family, church, neighborhood, and voluntary association, is intermediary institutions: the structures through which meaning, value, and identity are formed, and which stand between an isolated individual and the impersonal scale of the modern state, the market, and the mass media. Notice that this is Tocqueville's remedy given a name. His voluntary associations are one species of the same genus.

Bellah's contribution is not a new structural claim. It is a linguistic one. Even a person who wants to act on behalf of something larger than himself may lack the words to describe why, because the culture's two dominant vocabularies were built for something else entirely. This is the linguistic dimension of the problem Putnam later documents empirically and Nisbet analyzes structurally: a culture in which the vocabulary of genuine community has weakened even where the desire for it has not.

📚 PART 3 · SORT THE LANGUAGE

Bellah's categories are easy to state and harder to apply. Judge each statement by the reasoning it actually offers, not by whether the person sounds nice.

⚡ TRY IT · WHICH LANGUAGE IS THIS?
Decide which moral language is doing the work in each statement.
1. "I serve on the volunteer fire company's board because these are my neighbors, and somebody has to do it, whether or not I ever need them myself."
2. "I stayed at the firm because the exit package was worth more than what I'd earn starting over somewhere else."
3. "I would do anything for my own family, but what happens in the next town over isn't really my concern."
4. "I left the job because it wasn't fulfilling anymore, and I owe it to myself to find work that actually feels authentic."
KEY TERMS
Utilitarian Individualism
One of Bellah's two dominant moral languages: rational self-interest, cost-benefit calculation, and personal advantage. Associated with figures like Benjamin Franklin and with the market economy.
Expressive Individualism
Bellah's second language: authentic self-expression, personal growth, emotional fulfillment. Associated with the Romantic tradition and the twentieth-century therapeutic culture.
Intermediary Institutions
Berger and Neuhaus's term (To Empower People, 1977) for family, church, neighborhood, and voluntary association: the institutions standing between the individual and the mega-structures of modern life, through which meaning and identity are formed.
📚 PART 4 · WHERE THEY ACTUALLY DISAGREE

It is tempting to treat Nisbet, Berger and Neuhaus, Bellah, and Putnam as four versions of one argument, and the convergence is real: all four locate the problem in the thinning of intermediary institutions, and all four trace the lineage back to Tocqueville. But they do not agree on the remedy. Nisbet's diagnosis is structural and somewhat pessimistic: once destroyed, these communities are not easily rebuilt by policy, because they depend on habits and beliefs a society cannot simply legislate back into existence. Berger and Neuhaus are more hopeful, and more policy-minded: they argue government can deliberately empower mediating structures rather than bypass them. Putnam's own later work is the most optimistic of the four, proposing specific civic reforms to rebuild social capital. Bellah is, in a sense, the most radical, because his diagnosis is linguistic rather than institutional: rebuilding an institution does no good if the people in it lack the words to describe why it matters, so his remedy is closer to recovering a moral vocabulary than to founding a new organization.

This week's virtue is courtesy, and this is where it belongs. These four thinkers share a diagnosis and disagree, sometimes sharply, about the cure. A serious reader takes each disagreement on its merits rather than treating dissent as disloyalty to the shared cause. That habit, disagreeing about the remedy without contempt for the person proposing it, is itself a small instance of the associational life all four writers are trying to defend.

▶  Decide in your notebook which of the four you find most convincing, and why the others fall short. Then open this.
There is no single right answer, and a strong response will engage the objection to whichever thinker it picks. A common objection to Nisbet is that his pessimism assumes traditional communities were healthier than they actually were, and that new forms of association (online communities, hybrid workplaces, chosen family) may be replacing rather than merely losing the old ones. A common objection to Bellah is that ordinary people may act communally even while describing their actions in individualist language, so the linguistic evidence may understate the reality. Neither objection is decisive. Both are worth taking seriously before you settle on the frameworks you will use in your Capstone.

💪 WHAT YOU NOW KNOW

You can now trace a single line of argument across a century and a half: Tocqueville names it, Nisbet extends it structurally, Berger and Neuhaus give it a policy vocabulary, Bellah gives it a linguistic diagnosis, and Putnam gives it numbers. This is what a mature intellectual tradition looks like from the inside: not one book settling a question, but successive thinkers testing, refining, and disagreeing about a premise that keeps proving itself worth arguing over. Wednesday turns from tracing that line to using it, on a community you choose.

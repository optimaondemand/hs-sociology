📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
You have spent two quarters learning to see. Today you point that skill at one real place.
The Community Flourishing Capstone is due at the end of Week 18. It is the course's culminating assignment, and it asks you to do with a real community exactly what this course has trained you to do with a text: read it carefully, apply a framework honestly, and reach a conclusion you can defend.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to state the Capstone's exact requirements and select a community and at least three of its institutions to evaluate.
2
I will be able to write a specific, arguable thesis about what my chosen community's condition reveals about the sociology of flourishing.
3
I will be able to match at least three classical sociologists and at least three primary sources from this course to the specific institutions I plan to evaluate.

Before you write a sentence, do the research. Gather information about your chosen community's key institutions: how families are structured, how authority is organized, how economic life functions, how education and religion operate, and how the community handles social change. Bring the observational habits this course has built in you, watching for patterns, tensions, and strengths, rather than settling for a first impression.

📚 PART 2 · PICK A COMMUNITY, HONESTLY

Think out loud before you commit. No answer here is graded or wrong; the point is to test a candidate community against the requirement before you invest time in research you cannot use.

⚡ THINK IT THROUGH
Which description best matches the community you are currently considering?
KEY TERMS FOR THE CAPSTONE
Social Flourishing
A condition in which a community's members are equipped to live well: human dignity, meaningful work, genuine relationship, moral formation, and civic participation are present and accessible. Not merely the absence of problems, but the positive presence of the goods that make life worth living.
Indicators of Flourishing
Observable features that serve as evidence of a community's health: civic participation, trust, associational density, family stability, economic participation, cultural vitality, intergenerational continuity. Which indicators you choose reflects a theoretical commitment about what flourishing consists in, so choose deliberately, not by default.
📚 PART 3 · MATCH INSTITUTION TO FRAMEWORK

Different institutions call for different classical frameworks. Flip each card to see one strong pairing. These are starting points, not the only correct match.

INSTITUTIONFamily and kinship
APPLYDurkheim's solidarity: does this family generate mechanical or organic bonds, and what happens under strain?
INSTITUTIONLocal economy or marketplace
APPLYWeber's rationalization, or Marx's alienation: does work here connect people to each other, or process them?
INSTITUTIONReligious or civic association
APPLYTocqueville's voluntary associations, or Durkheim on religion: what does membership actually bind people to?
INSTITUTIONHow the community handles change
APPLYTonnies's Gemeinschaft/Gesellschaft, or Putnam's social capital: is change absorbed by existing bonds, or does it erode them?
📚 PART 4 · THE QUESTION THIS COURSE HAS BEEN ASKING ALL ALONG

Look back at what the semester has actually traced. Tocqueville named the problem: what stands between the individual and the state, and what happens to a democratic people when the space in between goes empty. Durkheim gave the mechanism a name, anomie, the condition of a person unmoored from the regulating group. Tonnies gave the transition a structure, Gemeinschaft giving way to Gesellschaft. Nisbet made the political stakes explicit: a person deprived of real community does not simply become isolated, he becomes available to whatever fills the vacuum, a mass movement or an unchecked state. Putnam supplied the evidence that the erosion is not theoretical but measured, in declining trust and declining membership across fifty years. This week's readings are the bookends: Tocqueville on the softening of manners under equality, and Tocqueville closing Volume I on the American principle of "unguided exertion" against the Russian principle of authority "centred in a single arm."

The Capstone is not a review of these thinkers. It is the moment the question stops being about nations and traditions in general and becomes a question about one particular, real place you can actually observe. What stands between the people in your chosen community and the impersonal forces (the state, the market, the algorithm) that would otherwise organize their lives for them? Is it strong, thin, or gone? That is the question your thesis should answer.

▶  Draft one sentence of your thesis in your notebook before you open this.
A strong thesis names the community, makes a specific and arguable claim (not merely "this community has strengths and weaknesses"), and points at the evidence that will carry it. A workable template: "In [community], [specific institution or condition] shows that [claim about flourishing], because [specific evidence], which [named sociologist]'s framework explains." Notice that the template forces you to commit to a position before you have written the body of the paper. If you cannot fill in the claim clause with something a reasonable person could disagree with, the thesis is not yet specific enough, and that is worth discovering now rather than in Week 18.

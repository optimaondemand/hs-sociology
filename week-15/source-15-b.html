WHAT TO READ
This reading is in a book, not on this page. Here is exactly where to start and stop.
THE BOOK
Max Weber, The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism. Translated by Talcott Parsons. Any printing of the Parsons translation works. If your library holds a different translation, that is fine too, but the chapter title below is the Parsons one.
CHAPTER
Chapter V, titled Asceticism and the Spirit of Capitalism. You are assigned only its closing section, not the whole chapter.
START AT
The sentence: “The Puritan wanted to work in a calling; we are forced to do so.” This comes after Weber's long discussion of the Puritan devotional writer Richard Baxter; you are picking the argument up at its turn toward the present.
STOP AT
The sentence ending: “…this nullity imagines that it has attained a level of civilization never before achieved.” Stop there. A closing methodological paragraph follows, about how this argument relates to a purely economic explanation of history; it is not part of this assignment.
HOW LONG
Roughly three to four pages, depending on your printing. This is the shortest reading of the quarter. Read it twice if the first pass feels quick; it rewards a second, slower reading.
WHAT TO SKIP
Everything before your starting sentence: Baxter's devotional writing, Weber's discussion of scripture and the danger of wealth, and his survey of Puritan attitudes toward property. That material sets up the argument; the assigned passage is where Weber states the argument's conclusion in his own voice.
Page numbers are useless here because they differ between printings, which is why the boundaries above are given by the words themselves. If the sentence you find first does not match, you are in the wrong place; check the chapter heading before reading further.

💡 WHERE THIS FITS
Wednesday's lesson spent a long time on George Ritzer's McDonaldization thesis without ever quoting the thinker Ritzer says he is applying. This is that thinker, in his own words, from the book Ritzer names as his source. You already met Weber on rationalization and bureaucracy in Week 11. This passage is where the idea began, in Weber's earlier study of the Protestant ethic, before he ever wrote about bureaucracy at all.
📖 WHY YOU ARE READING THIS

Across the whole book, Weber has been arguing that the Puritan idea of a religious calling, the belief that disciplined, methodical work in one's worldly occupation was a sign and duty of salvation, helped produce the discipline modern capitalism required. This closing passage asks the uncomfortable question that argument sets up: what happens to that discipline once the religious belief that justified it fades, but the discipline itself does not?

The passage moves in four steps.

1
Name the reversal. The Puritan chose disciplined work as a religious duty. We no longer choose it; the economic order simply requires it of anyone born into it, believer or not.
2
Explain how the requirement became inescapable. Weber traces how the discipline built, over generations, an enormous interlocking economic system that now runs on its own logic rather than on anyone's belief.
3
Give the image. A garment that could once be put on or taken off, worn lightly for religious reasons, becomes something closer to a container with no door.
4
Risk a prophecy, and mark it as a risk. Weber imagines where this could lead if the underlying spirit never returns in some new form. Notice how carefully he hedges the prediction rather than simply asserting it.
🎯 READ FOR THESE FOUR QUESTIONS
  1. Ritzer's four dimensions of McDonaldization are efficiency, calculability, predictability, and control. Find the sentence or image in your assigned passage that comes closest to describing something like control. Is Ritzer's application of Weber's idea a fair extension, or a different claim wearing Weber's vocabulary?
  2. Weber says the Puritan chose the discipline of work; we are forced into it. What exactly changed between those two conditions, according to the passage? Is choice the right word for what the Puritan had, given how strongly Weber says his religious anxiety compelled him?
  3. What is the "iron cage" actually made of, in Weber's account: is it a set of laws, a set of institutions, a set of habits, or something else? Be precise; the passage supports a more specific answer than "capitalism in general."
  4. Weber's closing image describes a possible future, not a certainty. What would have to be true for his prediction to turn out wrong? Name a real, observable kind of evidence that would count against it.
🔍 THREE SENTENCES TO FIND AND MARK

Three sentences carry this passage. They are described below rather than printed, because finding them yourself is part of the work and because you should be reading them in the edition rather than here. Mark each one in your copy, then read the note underneath it.

1. The reversal. Near the start of the closing section, find the short sentence that sets what the Puritan wanted against what we are forced to do. It is one line and it turns on those two verbs.

Those two verbs mark the whole distance the chapter has travelled: from a freely embraced religious duty to an economic necessity nobody chose and nobody can simply opt out of. Everything else in the passage is Weber explaining how that distance got covered.

2. The image that names the idea. Weber first describes the Puritans holding their possessions loosely, like a light garment that can be thrown off at any moment. A sentence shortly after that turns the garment into something a person cannot remove. Find it.

A cloak is worn by choice and removed by choice. A cage is not. Weber is not saying capitalism swapped one garment for another. He is saying the relationship changed, from something a person wears to something a person is enclosed by. Ask yourself whether his image implies that anyone locked a door on purpose, or whether a structure can close around people with nobody intending it.

3. The prophecy. In the final pages Weber imagines what may remain if the ascetic spirit never returns in some new form. Find the sentence that names a two-part human type, one half hollowed of purpose and the other of feeling, and then delivers a hard verdict on a civilisation that mistakes that condition for progress.

Read the two halves of that type against each other. One has narrowed his competence to a fragment of what work could be, with no larger purpose attached. The other has kept the appetite for pleasure and lost whatever gave pleasure meaning. Weber's harshest word is aimed at neither failure by itself. It is aimed at the self-satisfaction of a civilisation that reads the loss as an achievement. You met this sentence in a different form back in Week 11; seeing it in its own context is the point of reading it here.

✏️ AFTER YOU READ

Write short answers to the four questions in your Sociological Journal document.

▶  Answer question 1 in your notebook first, then open this.
Weber's closest image to Ritzer's "control" is the cage itself: a structure that constrains behavior regardless of what any individual inside it wants. But there is a real gap worth naming. Weber is describing an economic order that shapes an entire civilization across generations, largely through impersonal market and institutional pressure. Ritzer is describing a specific, designed business model, the fast-food restaurant, deliberately engineered by named organizations for calculability and control. Ritzer's application is defensible as an extension of Weber's logic to a smaller and more intentional case, but it is an extension, not a restatement. A student who calls them "the same idea" has missed the difference between a structure nobody designed and one somebody built on purpose.
▶  Name one piece of evidence against Weber's prophecy, then open this.
Weber himself hedges this passage more than readers usually remember; he is imagining one possible future, not announcing a law of history. Evidence against it would include any large-scale return of a genuinely animating purpose attached to work, whether religious or otherwise, that is not simply purpose invented after the fact to justify labor already being performed. Evidence for it would include the widespread modern experience of work as instrumentally necessary but not meaningfully chosen, which is close to what Weber predicts. Either way, treat this as an empirical question with a real answer, not a mood to agree or disagree with. That is exactly the discipline Durkheim asked of you in Week 3.

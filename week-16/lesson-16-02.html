📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
If virtue is not innate, where does it actually come from, and what happens to it when the institutions that used to supply it weaken?
Yesterday's lesson asked what happens when a society disagrees about the source of moral authority. Today's asks a related but different question: however that disagreement gets settled, how does any single person come to have character at all?

2

📚 PART 1 · THE FORMATION QUESTION

One of the deepest questions in social theory is what we might call the formation question: how do human beings develop the character, values, and moral sensibilities that guide their social lives? Where do virtues come from? What produces, in actual flesh-and-blood human beings, the dispositions that Aristotle identified as the material of the good life and the good society? This question was central to ancient philosophy, where Plato and Aristotle's answer runs through habituation, practice, and a well-ordered community, and it has remained central ever since. A sociologist of religion, Christian Smith, spent much of his career asking this question about American adolescents in the early twenty-first century, and his answers are both empirically grounded and theoretically important.

Smith's work, particularly his 2009 book Souls in Transition and his 2011 book Lost in Transition, documents through extensive interview research the moral and spiritual lives of young Americans. What he finds is sobering: widespread moral confusion, the prevalence of what he calls moralistic therapeutic deism (a vague sense that a God exists who wants people to be nice and to feel good about themselves) rather than robust religious commitment or serious moral formation, and the disconnection of many young people from the communities, practices, and traditions that have historically provided the conditions for genuine moral development.

📚 PART 2 · KEY TERMS
Moralistic Therapeutic Deism
A term for the dominant de facto religious worldview a sociologist of religion found among American teenagers in the 2000s: that a God exists who created the world, wants people to be nice and to be happy, that the central goal of life is to feel good about yourself, that God is not involved in daily life except when needed, and that good people go to heaven. A functional replacement for traditional religious commitment that lacks its demands and its formative power.
Moral Formation
The process by which a person's character, values, and moral dispositions are developed through practice, habituation, and participation in formative communities and traditions. The classical account (Aristotle, later Aquinas) is that virtues are formed through repeated virtuous action within a community that models and rewards those virtues.
📚 PART 3 · HOW A CHARACTER GETS BUILT
ARISTOTLE'S MECHANISM
The same loop produces virtue or vice. Nothing about the mechanism decides which.
STEP 1
Repeated acts
We become just by doing just acts, brave by doing brave acts. Not by being taught what justice is.
→
STEP 2
Habit
The acts, repeated, become a settled disposition. This is what "ethike" (moral virtue) means: formed by a variation on "ethos," habit.
→
STEP 3
State of character
A stable disposition that now governs future action, and is felt as pleasure or pain when tested.
↻
Practice virtuous acts
Produces a virtuous state of character, which produces more virtuous acts.
Practice vicious acts
Produces a vicious state of character, which produces more vicious acts. Same mechanism, opposite direction.
Aristotle's own line for this: "it is from the same causes and by the same means that every virtue is both produced and destroyed." A community that rewards vice will form vicious people. This is the empirical question a sociologist of religion went looking for.

Smith's account of moral formation is deeply consonant with Aristotle's. Aristotle argued, in the Nicomachean Ethics (Book II), that virtues are not innate: they are formed through habituation. We become just by doing just acts; we become courageous by practicing courage. The character of the community in which this habituation occurs matters enormously. Aristotle's account requires a particular kind of community: not merely an aggregation of individuals with compatible interests but a genuine polis with shared commitments to the good life, shared practices of civic and personal virtue, and shared institutions that transmit those commitments across generations.

Smith's empirical research suggests that the communities capable of providing this kind of formation are increasingly rare in contemporary American life. The institutions that historically performed this function, churches, families, schools, civic organizations, are under pressure from the same forces of rationalization, individualism, and consumerism that Weber, Durkheim, and Putnam each analyzed from a different angle. The result, on this account, is a generation of young people who are, by and large, morally adrift: not malicious, but without the formed dispositions, the shared vocabulary of virtue, or the communal supports that serious moral agency requires.

📍 CASE IN POINT · SPARTA AND CRETE: LAWGIVERS WHO BUILT HABITS

Aristotle’s claim that character is built by habit is not only about individuals. “This is confirmed by what happens in states; for legislators make the citizens good by forming habits in them, and this is the wish of every legislator, and those who do not effect it miss their mark.” In the Politics he names the cities he has in mind. Sparta and Crete kept “common meals, whereby the legislator has made property common,” and he holds that “the special business of the legislator is to create in men this benevolent disposition.”

The common meal is a habit-forming institution in the lesson’s sense: a daily practice, required by law, that trains a disposition toward shared use without abolishing private ownership. Aristotle reports the two cities as a comparative observer and does not say the arrangement succeeded; elsewhere in the same book he criticizes both. The question the mechanism raises is his own: which habits does a community’s ordinary routine actually form?

Source: Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics II.1 (trans. W. D. Ross, 1925) and Politics II.5 (trans. Benjamin Jowett); both public domain.
📚 PART 4 · TEST IT: HOW FORMATIVE IS THIS COMMUNITY?

Aristotle's own criteria: sustained practice, an explicit standard, and accountability to it over time. Place each community on that spectrum, and notice that the honest answer for the last one is genuinely contested.

⚡ PLACE IT ON THE SPECTRUM
How much capacity does each community have to form virtue by Aristotle's own criteria: sustained practice, a shared standard, and real accountability to it?
1. A team that practices together daily, shares an explicit standard of excellence, and holds members accountable to it for years.
2. An anonymous online forum where members interact once and never again.
3. A workplace with high turnover, no shared standard of conduct beyond legal compliance, and little continuity of relationships.
4. A multigenerational congregation with shared practices, explicit teaching on conduct, and long-term relationships of mutual accountability.
5. A large public high school with thousands of students, formal character-education lessons, but little sustained relationship between any given adult and student.
📚 PART 5 · MEET THE MECHANISM, ONE PIECE AT A TIME

Four questions Aristotle's own text raises about itself. Predict an answer before you flip each card.

⚡ TAP EACH CARD
Read the front, commit to a guess, then tap to check it against Aristotle's own resolution.
Why doesn't Aristotle think virtue is natural, the way sight is?
A thing that acts one way by nature cannot be trained to act another way. A stone thrown upward ten thousand times still falls. So if virtue could arise by nature alone, no amount of bad habituation could ever destroy it, and experience shows that it can. His answer: we are adapted by nature to receive virtue, but made perfect by habit.
How can you become just by doing just acts, if doing an act justly seems to already require being just?
Aristotle grants the puzzle and answers with three conditions: the agent must have knowledge, must choose the act for its own sake, and must act from a firm and settled character. Early on, a person can do a just act without meeting all three (a child obeying a rule, say). Repetition under guidance is how the act comes to be done from the settled character that finally makes the person, and not just the act, just.
What does he mean by using pleasure and pain as a "sign" of character?
Someone who abstains from excess and is annoyed by the abstinence is not yet temperate, only compliant. Someone who abstains and takes no pain in it, or even delights in it, has the settled disposition. The feeling that accompanies an act, not just the act, is the evidence of what has actually been formed.
If virtue is formed by habitual action inside a community, what should that predict about a generation raised largely outside such communities?
Roughly what a sociologist of religion later reported finding: moral confusion rather than settled vice, since confusion is what an unformed disposition looks like from outside. That is a real point in favor of Aristotle's framework, though it is worth naming the objection: young people still form habits somewhere, in teams, jobs, and friend groups, so the finding may say less about whether formation happens and more about which institutions are doing it now.

💪 WHAT YOU NOW KNOW

Character is not a possession a person is born with or without. On this account it is built, act by repeated act, inside communities that supply a standard and enforce it long enough for the standard to become a disposition. That mechanism does not depend on any one society's settling its disagreement about where moral authority ultimately comes from, which is the question yesterday's lesson raised. It only requires that some community, somewhere, keeps doing the patient work of habituation. Whether enough communities still do is an empirical question, and it is the one this week leaves you holding.

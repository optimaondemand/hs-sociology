📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Does living among strangers change the kind of mind you have?
Simmel thought it must. A city asks something of its residents that a village never does: it asks them to be surrounded by people they will never know and to keep functioning anyway.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain Simmel's account of the metropolitan blasé attitude as an adaptation, not a character flaw, and apply it to an unfamiliar case.
2
I will be able to reconstruct Wirth's argument that urbanism weakens primary group ties, and state precisely what evidence would have counted against it.
3
I will be able to evaluate Jane Jacobs's objection to Wirth and decide, with reasons, whether density destroys community or produces a different form of it.
📚 PART 1 · THE STRANGER
TWO WAYS OF KNOWING THE PEOPLE AROUND YOU
Simmel's starting contrast. The city does not simply have more people in it; it changes what "knowing someone" means.
THE VILLAGE
You know nearly everyone you meet, often for your whole life.
Your emotional attention is spent on a small, stable set of people.
Behavior is predictable because it is personally known, not because it is regulated.
VS
THE METROPOLIS
You are surrounded by strangers whose behavior you cannot predict from personal knowledge.
Stimulation arrives constantly, from every direction, all day.
The mind adapts by cultivating a protective reserve: the blasé attitude.
Simmel's claim: the blasé attitude is not indifference to people. It is what a mind does so it does not have to feel the full weight of every stranger it passes.
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Picture moving from a town of six hundred people to a city of six million. What would surprise you most in the first week?

In 1903, the German sociologist Georg Simmel published a short essay titled “The Metropolis and Mental Life.” It begins with an observation that felt startling at the time and now seems obvious: city life requires a different kind of mind than village life. The village dweller knows everyone; her emotional and intellectual resources can be devoted to the full particulars of relationships with a small number of people she has known for years. The city dweller knows almost no one; she is surrounded by strangers whose behavior she cannot predict from personal knowledge. The city is stimulating beyond all prior human experience, and the mind's response to that over-stimulation is not breakdown but adaptation: the development of what Simmel calls a “blasé attitude,” a cultivated emotional reserve that protects the metropolitan individual from the overwhelming demands of urban life. He does not feel less; he protects himself from feeling too much.

This is sociology as literary insight: a careful observation about a pattern of individual behavior that is actually a social fact in Durkheim's sense, produced by the social conditions of city life and distributed across all metropolitan individuals regardless of their personal psychology. Simmel's essay raises questions that are as live now as they were in 1903: what does the city do to community? What does it do to the self? And what do we lose, as well as gain, when we trade the village for the metropolis?

📚 KEY TERMS
Urbanization
The process by which an increasing share of a population comes to live in cities. Modern urbanization is tied to industrialization: the factory system drew rural populations into cities, transforming both the cities and the rural communities they drew from.
Metropolitan Blasé Attitude
Simmel's term for the cultivated emotional reserve city life produces: responding to unfamiliar stimuli with intellectualized detachment rather than emotional engagement. A protective adaptation to overstimulation, not a character flaw.
Urbanism as a Way of Life
Louis Wirth's concept (1938): the urban environment does not merely house social life but actively shapes it, producing anonymity, secondary relationships, heterogeneity, and loose social bonds as characteristic features of city life.
📚 PART 2 · THE SOCIOLOGY OF URBAN SPACE

The Chicago School of Sociology, active in the early twentieth century, pioneered the systematic empirical study of urban social life. Its leading figures, Robert Park, Ernest Burgess, and Louis Wirth, argued that the city was not merely a larger version of the village but a qualitatively different social environment. Its scale and density produced a different kind of social organization: anonymous, specialized, functionally differentiated. Park argued that the city sorted its inhabitants into “natural areas” characterized by distinctive social types: the bohemian quarter, the slum, the business district, the suburb. This spatial organization was not accidental. It reflected the social forces produced by urban competition and differentiation.

Wirth's “Urbanism as a Way of Life” (1938) argued that the distinctive features of urban life, size, density, heterogeneity, systematically weakened the primary group ties (family, neighborhood, church) that had provided social solidarity in pre-urban communities. The city produced secondary relationships, shallow, functionally specific, easily dissolved, in place of primary ones: deep, multifaceted, enduring. The result was social disorganization: not chaos, but a weakening of the normative bonds that had regulated social life in smaller communities.

📍 CASE IN POINT · EAST LONDON, 1861 TO 1881, COUNTED BY CHARLES BOOTH

Charles Booth studied a city by counting it. Working from the census returns and his own inquiry, he found that in East London and Hackney “the whole population of the district increased in the 20 years from 654,000 to 879,000.” Compared with England as a whole, the district was “strong in manufacture, transport, dealing, and industrial service,” and within manufacture the surplus came from “tailoring, bootmaking, and cabinet-making.” The same tables recorded loss: shipbuilders “decreased from 4877 to 3684,” their share of the employed population “falling from 1.63 to .94.”

This is urbanization observed as a population rather than as an impression. Booth’s categories are his own, drawn up in 1889, and the figures tell the story of waning as well as prospering trades, which is his phrase. Set his method beside Simmel’s: one asks what the metropolis does to a mind, the other asks who lives there and what they do for a living.

Source: Charles Booth, Life and Labour of the People in London, “The Trades of East London,” ch. I (1889), English original (public domain).
📚 PART 3 · CRITIQUE AND REVISION

Wirth's account was important but also somewhat overdrawn. Subsequent empirical research showed that urban communities do maintain primary group ties, that neighborhoods do develop genuine solidarity, and that the city is not simply a machine for dissolving community. Jane Jacobs, in The Death and Life of Great American Cities (1961), offered a powerful counter-argument: well-designed city streets, with their mixed uses and active sidewalks, produce their own distinctive form of social life, not less than rural community life but differently constituted. The city street, at its best, creates what Jacobs calls “sidewalk ballet”: a complex, spontaneous social choreography that generates safety, public trust, and genuine community among people who do not know each other as personal friends.

⚡ TRY IT · WHOSE ACCOUNT FITS?
For each observation, decide which theorist's framework it best supports. Judge by the mechanism each theorist proposes, not just the vocabulary.
1. On a busy residential street, shopkeepers, stoop-sitters, and dog walkers keep an informal watch on the block all day without ever organizing to do so.
2. A commuter steps around a loud argument on the platform without breaking stride or changing expression, having seen dozens like it that month.
3. A city's arts scene, its immigrant groceries, and its finance firms each cluster in a different, recognizable district.
4. A resident has lived beside the same family for ten years and knows only their names and that they keep to themselves.

💪 WHAT YOU NOW KNOW

A city changes the terms of social life rather than simply removing community from it. Simmel located the change in the individual mind, Wirth located it in the weakening of primary bonds, and Jacobs showed that dense, well-designed urban space can generate its own real form of solidarity among strangers. None of the three is simply wrong. Each is describing a genuine mechanism, and the honest position is to ask, of any particular street or city, which mechanism is doing the most work there.

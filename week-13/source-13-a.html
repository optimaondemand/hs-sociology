WHAT TO READ
This reading is in a book, not on this page. Here is exactly where to start and stop.
THE BOOK
Emile Durkheim, The Rules of Sociological Method. Translated by W. D. Halls. Any printing of the Halls translation works. If your library holds a different translation, that is fine too, but the wording quoted below is the Halls wording.
START AT
Chapter III, titled Rules for the Distinction of the Normal from the Pathological. The chapter opens: “Every sociological phenomenon, just as every biological phenomenon…”
STOP AT
The end of the chapter. It closes on Durkheim's own defense against a misreading he expects: the chapter's last words are “…to speak of them in language that is not commonly used.”
HOW LONG
Roughly fifteen to twenty pages depending on your printing. Budget thirty to forty minutes.
WHAT TO SKIP
Nothing is required reading, but you may move quickly through two technical stretches: an early passage on how a fact's generality connects to what Durkheim calls "the conditions of existence of the species," and a long comparative survey of crime rates across different societies used to establish that crime is universal. Both support the argument. Neither is where the argument actually turns. Do not skip the discussion of Socrates or the closing pages defending the argument against the charge that it excuses crime; those are the sections the guide questions below depend on.
Page numbers are useless here because they differ between printings, which is why the boundaries above are given by chapter and by the words themselves. If the first words you find do not match, you are in the wrong chapter.

💡 WHERE THIS FITS
Monday's lesson gave you the conclusion: crime is normal, and Durkheim means the word technically rather than as praise. This chapter is where he earns that claim, and the order of his argument matters. He does not start from crime at all. He starts from a general question in the philosophy of science, what it means for anything to be "normal," and only then applies the answer to the hardest possible test case. Watch him build the general rule before he uses it.
📖 WHY YOU ARE READING THIS

This is the chapter where Durkheim's most provocative claim gets its actual defense rather than its headline. Read it as a methodological argument as much as a substantive one. He is not only saying something about crime. He is showing what it means, in his science, for anything at all to count as normal.

The chapter moves in five steps. Track them and the argument holds together.

1
Define normal in general, before touching crime. A trait is normal for a species if it is the trait most commonly found, given that species' present stage of development. Normal is not a synonym for good.
2
Set the test: generality. Whether a fact is normal is settled by how widespread it is across a society at its current stage, not by anyone's preference for how things ought to be.
3
Apply the test to the hardest case available. Crime is found in every society that has ever been observed. By his own test, that alone makes it normal, whatever anyone feels about it.
4
Push the claim further: crime is not merely present but useful. A society that somehow eliminated its worst offenses would not become crime-free. It would simply reclassify smaller faults as the new crimes, because moral sensitivity rises as serious wrongdoing becomes rare.
5
Head off the obvious objection. Watch for the moment he insists that calling crime normal is not the same as excusing it. This is the point students most often skip past, and it is the point the guide questions below test directly.
🎯 READ FOR THESE FOUR QUESTIONS
  1. What does Durkheim mean by "normal" in this context? Is he using the word in a statistical sense, a functional sense, or a normative sense?
  2. Durkheim argues that if crime were eliminated, the community's moral sensitivity would increase and previously minor infractions would become crimes. Is this a convincing argument? Can you think of historical evidence for or against it?
  3. Durkheim uses Socrates as his example of a moral deviant who was in fact ahead of his society's moral development. Is this a good example? Does it support his argument?
  4. Does Durkheim's argument that crime is normal imply that we should not punish criminals? How do the two halves of his argument, crime is normal, and punishment serves social functions, fit together?
🔍 THREE SENTENCES TO FIND AND MARK

Three sentences carry the chapter's central move. Find each one in your copy and mark it, then read the note underneath.

1. The minimal claim. Before he pushes further, Durkheim states the modest version of the argument, the part that is hardest to deny:

“In the first place, crime is normal because it is completely impossible for any society entirely free of it to exist.”

Rules of Sociological Method, Chapter III

Notice what this sentence does and does not claim. It does not say crime is good. It says universality is itself a fact requiring explanation, and "no society has ever managed to eliminate it" is a claim about evidence, not about values. Ask yourself what kind of evidence could actually overturn it.

2. The stronger claim. Having established universality, Durkheim pushes to the sentence that made the chapter famous:

“To classify crime among the phenomena of normal sociology is not merely to declare that it is an inevitable though regrettable phenomenon arising from the incorrigible wickedness of men; it is to assert that it is a factor in public health, an integrative element in any healthy society.”

Rules of Sociological Method, Chapter III

The semicolon marks the exact place his claim jumps from modest to radical. Before it: crime is inevitable, an unhappy but tolerable fact. After it: crime is useful, actively contributing to a healthy society's functioning. Most objections to Durkheim attack the second half without noticing he has already conceded, and moved past, the first.

3. The disclaimer. Late in the chapter, Durkheim addresses the misreading almost everyone reaches for:

“It would be a peculiar distortion to represent our thinking as an apologia for crime.”

Rules of Sociological Method, Chapter III

He follows this with an analogy to physical pain: detested by every organism that feels it, and still a normal, useful feature of a healthy nervous system. Decide for yourself whether the analogy actually rescues him. A critic could reply that pain has an obvious function (it protects the body from damage), while the function of any single crime is much harder to specify. Is that a fair objection, or does it miss that Durkheim's claim was always about crime as a category, not about any one criminal act?

✏️ AFTER YOU READ

Write short answers to the four questions in your Sociological Journal document.

▶  Decide in your notebook whether Socrates is a fair example of Durkheim's point, then open this.
By Athenian law, Durkheim points out, Socrates really was a criminal, and his conviction was, by the standards of Athenian law, entirely just. His crime, an independence of thought that unsettled the city's traditional pieties, is exactly what Durkheim's social-change function describes: a violation that later ages judged as moral progress. The example is strong precisely because it does not soften the original verdict. Durkheim is not claiming Athens was wrong to convict Socrates by its own lights. He is claiming that the very existence of people willing to be convicted for their convictions is part of how a society's morality is able to move at all. A fair objection: Socrates is a single, famous case, chosen because history vindicated him. For every Socrates, are there a hundred deviants history simply forgot, whose "independence of thought" turned out to be nothing more than error? Durkheim's argument needs some deviance to be forward-looking on average, not every act of deviance to be admirable.
▶  Answer question 4 in your notebook first, then open this.
They fit together because "normal" in Durkheim's sense describes a fact's place in the social order, not its moral standing or its treatment. A disease can be a normal part of an ecosystem's biology and still be worth curing in the individual patient; a species-level fact about deviance and an act-level fact about a specific offender are two different levels of description. Durkheim can consistently hold that crime as a category is inevitable and even functional for society while also holding that any particular criminal should be punished, because punishment is doing separate work: reaffirming the collective conscience the individual act violated, which you met in Monday's lesson as boundary maintenance. The chapter's own closing pages make this explicit when Durkheim insists he is not writing an apologia for crime.

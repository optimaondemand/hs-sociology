📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Is community what completes us, or what contains us?
Four thinkers, two thousand years apart, gave four different answers. What they decided about human nature determined everything they went on to say about law, government, and social order. Get their starting points clear and the rest of this course becomes an argument you can follow.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to reconstruct each of the four accounts of human nature as an argument rather than a slogan, and name the premise each one rests on.
2
I will be able to derive from each account what it commits its author to about how a society must then be organised.
3
I will be able to identify the single premise a critic would have to reject in order to defeat Aristotle's argument, and do the same for Hobbes's.
📚 PART 1 · THE QUESTION BEHIND ALL THE QUESTIONS

Before there was sociology, there was philosophy. And before sociology asked what holds society together, philosophy asked a prior question: what kind of creature is the human being, and what does that nature imply about how humans should live together?

The answer to the first question shapes every possible answer to the second. If humans are naturally social, then community is the fulfillment of human nature. If humans are naturally solitary and competitive, then community is an artificial construction imposed on an unsociable animal. These two starting points lead to entirely different theories of politics, law, and social order.

The four thinkers in this lesson span two thousand years of Western thought and represent the major positions in the debate. Here is the whole argument on one line.

FOUR ANSWERS TO ONE QUESTION
What does society do to human nature?
SOCIETY SPOILS SOMETHING THAT WAS ALREADY FINE
SOCIETY COMPLETES SOMETHING NATURE INTENDED
Rousseau
1755 · GENEVA
Natural man is solitary, self-sufficient, and content. Civilization introduced property, comparison, and envy. Society is the corruption.
Hobbes
1651 · ENGLAND
Humans are naturally competitive and roughly equal, so no one is safe. Society is the cage that makes civilization possible.
Locke

Aristotle

📚 PART 2 · THE WORDS THREE OF THEM SHARE

Three of these four thinkers argue using the same two ideas. Get the terms straight before you meet the arguments.

State of Nature
A thought experiment describing what human life would be like outside of organized society and law. Hobbes, Locke, and Rousseau all use it. They reach very different conclusions from it, which is what makes it worth arguing about.
Social Contract
The idea that legitimate political authority comes from an agreement, stated or implied, among individuals to form a society and submit to common rules.
Political Animal (zoon politikon)
Aristotle's term for the human being, from the Greek zoion (living creature) and politikon (of the city). For Aristotle humans are not merely capable of political life. They require it in order to flourish. Notice that Aristotle needs no state of nature and no contract. That is the measure of how differently he begins.
📚 PART 3 · THE FOUR ACCOUNTS

Aristotle: the social animal

Writing in Athens around 350 BC, Aristotle opens the Politics (I.2) with the most elegant statement of one side of this debate: man is by nature a political animal. He means it literally. The human being, unlike the bee or the ant, does not merely live in a community. A human is constituted for community in a way that makes solitary life either less than human or more than human. A person who can live alone, Aristotle says, must be either a beast or a god. This is not sentiment. It is a claim about human nature resting on a premise Aristotle states plainly at Politics I.2: the nature of any thing is what that thing is when fully developed. Grant him that premise and the conclusion follows, because the city is the condition in which human capacities reach their completion. Reject it and the argument loses its footing. That premise, not the famous phrase, is where the argument is actually vulnerable.

Hobbes: the unsocial animal

Thomas Hobbes, writing in the chaos of the English Civil War in 1651, reached the opposite conclusion. In the state of nature, without law or government, human life is solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short (Leviathan XIII). Humans are not naturally social. They are naturally competitive. Each person pursues power as a means of securing survival, and because all are roughly equal in strength and cunning, no one is safe. The result is a condition of universal war, or at minimum universal suspicion. Society on this account is not the fulfillment of human nature but the cage that makes civilization possible. Hobbes's sovereign, the Leviathan, is the power strong enough to frighten individuals into cooperation. Remove that power and civilization dissolves.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Hobbes's war of every one against every one does not mean people are constantly fighting. It means a permanent condition of mutual suspicion in which violence is always possible and nobody can safely assume otherwise. Long stretches of no actual combat are entirely compatible with it. Readers who miss this take Hobbes to be saying something obviously false, and then stop listening to him.

Locke: the rational bargainer

John Locke accepted Hobbes's starting point but rejected his conclusions. Writing forty years after Hobbes, Locke argued in the Second Treatise (§6) that the state of nature is not a war of all against all but a condition of natural freedom governed by a law of reason that all persons can in principle recognize. People are not naturally peaceful, but they are naturally rational. They can perceive that their long-term interests are better served by cooperation than by conflict, and they can therefore consent to form a government without surrendering all their rights to a sovereign. For Locke, government is a trust. When it violates the terms of that trust by threatening the life, liberty, and property of its citizens, the people have the right to dissolve it.

Rousseau: natural innocence and a corrupting society

Jean-Jacques Rousseau, writing in 1755 in Part One of the Discourse on Inequality, introduced a radical inversion. Natural man, Rousseau argued, is not Hobbes's predator, Locke's bargainer, or Aristotle's political animal. Natural man is solitary, self-sufficient, and content. He has few desires and the means to satisfy them. He is not aggressive because he does not need what others have. He is not social because he does not yet have language, property, or the comparisons that create vanity and envy.

It is civilization, not nature, that corrupts. The invention of agriculture, private property, and settled society introduced inequality, comparison, and the endless competition for status that makes human beings miserable. Where Hobbes saw the state of nature as hell and society as rescue, Rousseau saw it the other way around.

📍 CASE IN POINT · THE AMERICAS IN THE STATE-OF-NATURE ARGUMENT

Two of this lesson’s thinkers point to the same continent when asked whether the state of nature ever existed. Hobbes writes that “the savage people in many places of America, except the government of small Families, the concord whereof dependeth on naturall lust, have no government at all” (Leviathan XIII). Locke, making a different point, writes that the lawmakers of “England, France or Holland, are to an Indian, but like the rest of the world, men without authority” (Second Treatise §9): a person outside a commonwealth is bound by the law of nature alone.

Neither passage names an observer or a source. Each treats a report about distant peoples as settled fact and builds on it. Rousseau, by contrast, labels his own account a construction: “Let us conclude that savage man, wandering about in the forests” opens a hypothesis, not a description of anyone. What would count as evidence for Hobbes’s claim, and does his chapter supply any?

Source: Hobbes, Leviathan I.XIII (1651); Locke, Second Treatise II §9 (1689); Rousseau, Discourse on Inequality, Part One (1755). All English originals or public-domain renderings.

📚 PART 6 · WHAT SOCIOLOGY INHERITS

Each of these four accounts leaves a legacy in sociology. The names in the right column are the ones you will spend the rest of this course with.

From this thinkerSociology inherits
AristotleDurkheim takes up the conviction that social bonds are real and constitutive, not merely useful arrangements people opt into.
RousseauMarx takes up the idea that civilization introduces forms of inequality and alienation that simpler conditions did not have.
HobbesWeber takes up the concern with power and domination, and with what makes obedience possible.

💪 WHAT YOU NOW KNOW

You now hold four incompatible answers to the same question, and you should not yet be sure which is right. That is the correct state to be in. Every thinker you meet from here on inherits one of these starting points, usually without saying so. When Durkheim insists that society is a reality in its own right, he is standing where Aristotle stood. When Marx describes what industrial life does to a worker, he is standing where Rousseau stood. Learn to hear the older argument underneath the newer one.

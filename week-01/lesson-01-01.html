📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Why did anyone need to invent a science of society?
Human beings have lived in societies for as long as there have been human beings. Yet the systematic study of society is barely two centuries old. Something happened in Europe between 1750 and 1850 that made people start asking, for the first time and with real urgency, what holds a society together. Today you will find out what that something was, and why the answer still matters.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain why sociology emerged in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries rather than at some earlier point in history.
2
I will be able to assign an unfamiliar research question to sociology, history, psychology, or economics, and defend the assignment by naming what each discipline attends to.
3
I will be able to trace sociology's founding questions to the specific institutional collapses that provoked them, and judge which of those questions remain open.

1838 · AUGUSTE COMTE COINS THE WORD
sociology
Latin socius, companion · Greek logos, study
What holds human societies together, and what happens when the bonds that hold them begin to fray?

In the summer of 1789, the French social order collapsed with a speed that no one had imagined possible. Within weeks, the Bastille had fallen, feudal obligations had been abolished by the National Assembly, and the king who had ruled by divine right was no longer sovereign in any meaningful sense. Within three years, he would be dead. The revolution that followed dismantled the parish, the guild, the aristocratic estate, and the monarchy that had organized French society for centuries. What replaced them was not yet clear.

Across the Channel, a different kind of revolution had been underway for decades. The Industrial Revolution was drawing millions of men, women, and children off the land and into factories in Manchester, Birmingham, and Leeds. Villages that had existed for five hundred years were emptying. Cities were swelling beyond any prior experience. The old patterns of work, family, neighborhood, and belief that had structured life for most of human history were dissolving into something new and not yet named.

It was in this context of double disruption that the discipline of sociology was born. The word itself was coined by Auguste Comte in 1838, in the fourth volume of his Cours de philosophie positive, from the Latin socius (companion, associate) and the Greek logos (reason, study). Comte had first called the projected science physique sociale, social physics, and the substitution is worth noticing: he wanted a term that did not borrow its authority from another discipline. But the question sociology asks is older than its name, and older than Comte. What holds human societies together, and what happens when the bonds that hold them begin to fray?

📚 PART 2 · THE WORDS YOU WILL NEED

Four terms carry this lesson. Tap each card to check yourself, then write all four into your notebook in your own words.

⚡ TAP TO REVEAL
Sociology
From the Latin socius (companion) and the Greek logos (study, reason). The scientific study of human society, social institutions, and social relationships.
⚡ TAP TO REVEAL
Social Science
A field of inquiry that applies systematic, empirical methods to the study of human behavior and social organization. Sociology is distinguished from philosophy by its insistence on evidence.
⚡ TAP TO REVEAL
Industrial Revolution
The transformation of production from household and craft industries to factory-based manufacturing, beginning in Britain around 1760 and spreading through the nineteenth century. One of the two great disruptions that created sociology.
⚡ TAP TO REVEAL
French Revolution
The political and social upheaval in France from 1789 to 1799 that overthrew the monarchy and the old social order. The other great disruption. Together with industrialization it created the crisis of social coherence that sociology attempts to understand.
📚 PART 3 · SOCIOLOGY AMONG THE SCIENCES

Sociology did not emerge in a vacuum. By the mid-nineteenth century, the natural sciences had achieved remarkable success by treating the natural world as a domain governed by discoverable laws. Physicists described the motion of planets; biologists described the development of organisms; chemists described the behavior of matter. The early sociologists asked whether the same approach could be applied to human society. Could the patterns of social life be studied with the same rigor that Newton had applied to the movement of planets?

Sociology answers yes, but with an important qualification. The object of study is not atoms or organisms but social facts, shared meanings, institutions, and the relationships between persons. The subject matter is more complex and in some ways more resistant to simple measurement than the material world. But it is not therefore beyond understanding. The task of sociology, as Durkheim would insist in the preface to the second edition of The Rules of Sociological Method (1901), is to treat social phenomena as things: external objects of inquiry that can be described, compared, and explained. You will read the chapter that argument comes from in Week 3, and it is worth arriving at it already suspicious of how much work the word things is doing.

Sociology also distinguishes itself from its neighboring disciplines. Each of the four asks a different kind of question about the same human world.

DisciplineWhat it fixes its attention on
HistoryParticular events in time. What happened, to whom, and in what order.
PsychologyThe individual mind. What a person perceives, wants, remembers, and decides.
EconomicsThe behavior of rational actors in markets. How scarce goods get allocated.
SociologyPatterns across societies and periods, and the social forces that shape individuals before and beyond any individual's awareness.
💡 CONNECTION
Where the economist sees a buyer and a seller, the sociologist sees a social structure that made that transaction possible, and that made those persons the kinds of buyers and sellers they are. Sociology insists that markets, rationality, and even the definition of self-interest are themselves socially produced. Keep this move in mind. You will use it in every week of this course.
⚡ TRY IT · WHOSE QUESTION IS IT?
Each question below is a real research question. Decide which discipline is most likely asking it. Work from the kind of thing each one attends to, not from the topic.
1. Why do suicide rates stay stable year after year within a country, yet differ sharply between countries?
2. In what order did the Bastille fall, feudal obligations end, and the monarchy lose its authority in 1789?
3. How did factory wages in Manchester respond when the supply of rural labor increased?
4. What does an individual worker feel and remember during the first week of a job in an unfamiliar city?
📚 PART 4 · WHY THE TIMING MATTERS

Sociology's birth in the nineteenth century is not incidental. The discipline's founding questions are the questions of a world in transition. What holds a society together when the old religious and political authorities lose their grip? How do individuals find meaning and belonging in a world of strangers? What makes institutions legitimate? Can social life be organized on rational principles, or does it depend on bonds that reason cannot fully explain?

These remain the questions of the twenty-first century. Sociology was born in a moment of disruption, and it continues to be the discipline most directly concerned with understanding disruption: what causes it, what it costs, and what might survive it.

▶  Try it in your notebook first: name one bond in your own community that is weaker now than it was a generation ago. Then open this.
There is no single correct answer here, and that is the point. What matters is whether you named something specific and observable rather than something abstract. A sociologist would push you one question further: is the bond you named actually weaker, or has it simply changed form? Comte and the thinkers who followed him spent their careers on that distinction. Hold your answer. You will return to it in Week 8 and again in your capstone.

💪 WHAT YOU NOW KNOW

Sociology is not a timeless subject that happened to get a name in 1838. It is a response to a specific crisis: two revolutions, political and economic, that pulled apart the arrangements holding European life together and left people asking what a society actually is. You now have the word, its roots, and the reason it was needed. Next you will meet four thinkers who disagreed profoundly about whether human beings are built for society at all.

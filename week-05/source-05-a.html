📖 WHY YOU ARE READING THIS

Monday's lesson introduced Berger's idea that sociology begins with a chosen habit of seeing through the surface of ordinary situations. Simmel's short essay applies exactly that vantage point to a person, not a method: someone who is part of a group without ever having been fully formed by it. Lesson 4.02 already used this figure, calling her "the stranger" and noting that she is close and remote at once. This essay is where Simmel actually makes the case.

Simmel is not describing a foreigner passing through, and he is not describing a full member of the group either. The stranger he means arrives, stays, and takes up a position inside the group's ordinary life while remaining structurally apart from it, which is precisely what lets her notice what long-time members have stopped being able to see.

WHAT TO READ
This reading is in a book, not on this page. Here is where to find it.
THE BOOK
Georg Simmel, "The Stranger." Translated by Kurt H. Wolff. Collected in The Sociology of Georg Simmel (Glencoe, IL: Free Press, 1950). The essay was originally a short excursus near the end of Simmel's Soziologie (1908); Wolff's anthology prints it as a short standalone chapter.
START AT
The opening paragraph, where Simmel introduces the stranger through the contrast between wandering and settling, and names the combination of nearness and distance that defines the type.
STOP AT
The essay's final paragraph, where Simmel draws out what this structural position lets the stranger see that a full member of the group cannot.
HOW LONG
A short essay, only a handful of pages in the Wolff anthology. Plan for a single sitting of twenty to thirty minutes, read slowly rather than quickly.
WHAT TO SKIP
Nothing. The essay is short enough to read in full in one sitting.

🎯 READ FOR THESE FOUR QUESTIONS
  1. Simmel's stranger is neither a total outsider nor a full member. How does his account of nearness and distance held together explain that middle position, and how is it different from simple foreignness?
  2. Simmel notes that many societies historically assigned trading, moneylending, or similar roles specifically to outsiders. Why would a group deliberately keep such functions in the hands of people who are not fully its own?
  3. Simmel describes the stranger's judgment as more "objective" than an insider's. What does he mean by objectivity here, and is it purchased at any cost to the stranger herself?
  4. Berger describes a method a sociologist chooses to adopt. Simmel describes a social position some people simply occupy. Where do these two accounts of the outsider's vantage point overlap, and where do they genuinely diverge?
✏️ AFTER YOU READ

Write short answers to the four questions in your Sociological Journal document. You will use question 4 directly in Thursday's journal entry.

▶  Write your answer to question 3 in your notebook first, then open this to compare your reasoning.
Simmel's point is not that the stranger is a better or more virtuous observer. It is structural: she was not raised inside the group's particular history of grudges, loyalties, and unstated assumptions, so she has less at stake in defending any one account of "how things are here." That freedom is exactly what a fully socialized member lacks, and it is also what makes the stranger useful for roles a member could not perform impartially, such as adjudicating a dispute between two established families. The cost is real. The same distance that produces objectivity also produces suspicion, because the group cannot fully predict someone whose loyalties were not formed inside it. Objectivity and vulnerability, on this account, come from the same fact about her position, not from two separate features of her character.

TEXT STUDY 5A
The Stranger: Near and Far at Once
Simmel, Exkurs über den Fremden (Excursus on the Stranger), in Soziologie, ch. IX (1908)
💡 CONTEXT
Simmel’s large Soziologie of 1908 studies the recurring forms of social interaction, wherever they appear. Its ninth chapter treats space and how groups arrange themselves in it, and it carries several short digressions. One of them, only a few pages long, describes a single social position: the person who is inside a group without having been formed by it. Simmel is not writing about any one people or period. He is describing a form, and the form explains what Lesson 5.01 calls the outsider’s vantage point.
📊 THE ARGUMENT AT A GLANCE
THREE POSITIONS TOWARD A GROUP
THE WANDERERArrives and leaves. No lasting relation to the group.
 
THE STRANGERArrives and stays. In the group, but not of it from the start.
 
THE NATIVEBelonged from the beginning. Formed by the group’s history.
↓
NEARNESS + DISTANCEPhysically close, socially remote: both at full strength
→
WHAT IT MAKES POSSIBLETrade, objectivity, confidences, freedom from local ties
WHAT IT COSTSSuspicion; being treated as a type rather than as a person
The same position produces both columns. They are one fact seen from two sides.
📖 THE ARGUMENT, SECTION BY SECTION
1
Neither wanderer nor native
CLAIMThe stranger is not the traveller who passes through. He is the one who arrives today and is still there tomorrow, keeping the possibility of leaving that he has never quite given up.
REASONHis place in the group is fixed by two facts: he did not belong to it from the beginning, and he brings into it qualities that did not arise inside it. Being a stranger is therefore a relation to the group, not the absence of one.
EXAMPLESimmel’s limiting case: the inhabitants of a distant star are not strangers to us, because they stand in no relation to us at all. Strangeness requires membership of a kind.
2
Nearness and distance in one relation
CLAIMEvery human relation mixes nearness and distance. In the stranger’s case both are at their sharpest: someone close at hand is socially remote, and someone remote is present in daily life.
REASONThe stranger lives among the group and deals with its members constantly, yet he does not share the history, kinship, and loyalties that bind them. That combination makes him a specific social type, and Simmel treats him as an element of the group itself, much as the poor are, rather than an outsider looking in.
3
The stranger as trader
CLAIMIn the history of economic life the stranger appears above all as the trader.
REASONA group that produces what it needs has no use for a middleman. Once it needs goods made beyond its borders, someone from outside supplies them. If that person settles, he lives by trade and exchange because he holds no land, in the literal sense and in the wider sense of a fixed place in the group’s network of property and kinship. His mobility is tied to money, which moves as easily as he does.
EXAMPLESimmel’s own historical example is the Jews of medieval and early modern Europe, often barred from owning land or entering guilds and so channelled into trade and lending. He presents this as a position imposed by the host society, not as a trait of a people. Lesson 4.02 lists the occupations such outsiders were often assigned.
4
Objectivity
CLAIMBecause he is not rooted in the group’s particular loyalties, the stranger can view its affairs with objectivity.
REASONObjectivity here is not indifference or non-participation. It is a distinct blend of distance and involvement: the stranger cares about outcomes but is not bound to any faction. For the same reason people tell him things they would hide from those closer to them.
EXAMPLESome Italian city-states brought in their chief judges from other cities, so that no local family or party tie would bend the verdict.
5
Freedom, and its danger
CLAIMThe stranger’s objectivity is a kind of freedom. He is less bound by habit, piety, and precedent, and he judges situations by more general standards.
REASONNothing in his own history commits him to the group’s customary way of seeing. The same freedom makes him suspect. When a group is shaken by unrest, those in power often blame outside agitators, casting the stranger as the hidden cause of a disturbance that grew from within.
6
An abstract relation
CLAIMWith a stranger, people share only general features, such as nationality, occupation, or common humanity, not the specific traits that tie natives to one another.
REASONA bond resting on general features could link either party to countless others, so it feels replaceable. That is why groups so often treat strangers as a category rather than as individuals. Simmel adds that a trace of this strangeness enters even close relations, once a person senses that what binds him to another is something many people share.
⚖️ THE DISTINCTIONS THAT CARRY THE ARGUMENT
WANDERER / STRANGERPassing through, versus settling while remaining unabsorbed.
OUTSIDER / MEMBERThe stranger is both: an element of the group, defined by not having been one from the start.
OBJECTIVE / INDIFFERENTInvolvement without partisanship, versus no involvement at all.
GENERAL / SPECIFICTies resting on what many share, versus ties resting on what only these people share.
POSITION / TRAITA place in a social structure, versus a quality of a person or people.
❓ THE STRONGEST OBJECTION

Simmel’s stranger is free of the host group’s loyalties, but real strangers carry loyalties of their own: to a home country, a family network, a religious community, trading partners elsewhere. Their judgment is not neutral. It is partial in a different direction. If so, the stranger’s objectivity is better described as a different set of blind spots, useful mainly because it does not coincide with the insiders’ set.

A modern reader may also resist the trader example, since linking a people to a role risks stereotype. Simmel’s defence is his method: he describes a position that exclusion created, and the position, not the people, is his subject. Whether a form can be studied apart from the history that produced it is a fair question to press.

🎯 FOUR QUESTIONS
  1. Simmel’s stranger is neither a total outsider nor a full member. How does his account of nearness and distance held together explain that middle position, and how is it different from simple foreignness?
  2. Simmel notes that many societies historically assigned trading, moneylending, or similar roles specifically to outsiders. Why would a group deliberately keep such functions in the hands of people who are not fully its own?
  3. Simmel describes the stranger’s judgment as more objective than an insider’s. What does he mean by objectivity here, and is it purchased at any cost to the stranger himself?
  4. Berger describes a method a sociologist chooses to adopt. Simmel describes a social position some people simply occupy. Where do these two accounts of the outsider’s vantage point overlap, and where do they genuinely diverge?
✏️ AFTER THE QUESTIONS
▶  Question 3: what kind of objectivity?
The point is structural, not moral. The stranger is not a better or more virtuous observer. He was not raised inside the group’s history of grudges, loyalties, and unstated assumptions, so he has less at stake in defending any one account of how things are done here. That freedom suits him for roles a member could not perform impartially, such as settling a dispute between two established families. The cost is real: the distance that yields objectivity also yields suspicion, because the group cannot fully predict someone whose loyalties were formed elsewhere. Objectivity and vulnerability come from the same fact about his position.

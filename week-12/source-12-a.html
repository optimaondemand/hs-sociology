TEXT STUDY 12A
Crime as a Normal Social Fact
Durkheim, Les Règles de la méthode sociologique (The Rules of Sociological Method), ch. III, Règles relatives à la distinction du normal et du pathologique (1895)
💡 CONTEXT
Durkheim’s 1895 book argues that social facts can be studied as things, from the outside, like the objects of any science. Chapter III faces the obvious follow-up. If sociology is a science, it needs a way to tell a healthy society from a sick one without smuggling in the writer’s preferences. The chapter builds that test, then applies it to the case most likely to break it: crime.
📊 THE ARGUMENT AT A GLANCE
HOW THE CHAPTER REACHES ITS CONCLUSION
1 · DEFINENormal means general: found across the species or society at its stage
→
2 · TESTJudge by how widespread a fact is, not by anyone’s ideal
→
3 · APPLYCrime appears in every society, so it is normal
↓
4 · PUSH FURTHERCrime is also useful
→
INDIRECT USEIts presence shows collective feeling is flexible enough to change
DIRECT USECan anticipate a morality that has not yet arrived
→
5 · CLARIFYPunishment is not a cure, and normal does not mean approved
Steps 1 to 3 establish that crime is normal. Steps 4 and 5 say what follows from that.
📖 THE ARGUMENT, SECTION BY SECTION
1
Normal means common to the type, at its stage
CLAIMA social fact is normal if it belongs to the most common forms found across a society of a given type at a given phase of development. Facts found in only a few cases, and seldom lasting even there, are exceptions, which Durkheim calls pathological.
REASONNo one can say what health or sickness is in the abstract. Every species has its own average type, and a trait is pathological only measured against that type. The same holds for societies: an institution or moral maxim is not good or bad in itself for every kind of society.
EXAMPLEA living species. What counts as healthy for one differs from what counts as healthy for another, and no species is healthier by being higher. Durkheim carries this picture over to social types.
2
Generality is the test, because a science studies what is there
CLAIMHow widespread a fact is decides whether it is normal. Sociology would not be a science of things if its criterion were what someone wants society to be.
REASONIf the desirable must be worked out by reasoning alone, imagination has no limit and the goal keeps receding. If the desirable is the healthy, and health is a definite state present in things, the task becomes finite: maintain the normal state, restore it when disturbed, and look for its new conditions when circumstances change.
CONSEQUENCEThe statesman’s role resembles a physician’s more than a visionary’s. He prevents illness by good hygiene and treats it when it appears. Durkheim also adds a second check, that a general fact be explained by the conditions of the society’s existence, so that mere apparent normality is not mistaken for the real thing.
3
Applied to crime: no society is free of it
CLAIMCrime is normal because a society without any crime cannot exist.
REASONCrime is an act that offends strong, sharply defined collective feelings. For such acts to stop, those feelings would have to be present in every individual consciousness, at full strength, without exception. Even if that were achieved, crime would not vanish. It would change form, because the same cause that dried up old sources of crime would open new ones.
EXAMPLEA community of exemplary people living in a perfect monastery. Crime as ordinary people know it would be unknown there, yet faults that look slight elsewhere would cause the scandal that real crimes cause elsewhere. Given authority to try and sanction its members, that community would treat those faults as crimes. The most scrupulous person judges his own small failings with a severity most people reserve for real offences.
4
The stronger claim: crime is useful
CLAIMCalling crime normal does more than concede that it is an inevitable evil. It places crime among the conditions of a healthy society, one of the things that help hold it together.
REASONThe indirect use follows from step 3. Crime can occur only where collective feelings are not so overwhelming that no one ever departs from them. That same margin is what lets morality and law change at all: feelings strong enough to stamp out every offence would also be too rigid to take a new shape. Crime is the price of that flexibility, and so is bound up with conditions a healthy society needs. The direct use goes further: crime not only keeps the way to necessary change open but sometimes prepares the change itself, so that a deviant act can be an early sign of the morality to come.
EXAMPLESocrates. Athenian law made him a criminal, and by that law the verdict stood. His independence of thought nonetheless helped prepare a new morality and faith that Athens needed, because its inherited traditions no longer suited its conditions of life. Durkheim adds that the pattern recurs: a freedom now taken for granted was first won by breaking the rule that forbade it.
5
What follows for punishment, and what does not follow
CLAIMIf crime is not a disease, punishment cannot be its cure, so the true function of punishment must be sought elsewhere. Yet calling crime normal does not mean society should stop abhorring it.
REASONPain is detested by every living creature, and it is still a normal and irreplaceable function of life. Detesting a thing and finding it a regular, even useful part of the order of things are compatible. The chapter leaves the positive account of punishment unfinished. Durkheim develops it in De la division du travail social, Book I, where repressive law expresses and strengthens the collective conscience.
EXAMPLEA disease may be a normal part of an ecosystem and still be worth curing in one patient. Facts about a category and facts about a single act sit at different levels.
⚖️ THE DISTINCTIONS THAT CARRY THE CHAPTER
NORMAL / GOODNormal describes a fact’s place in a society’s regular pattern. It is not a verdict on whether the fact is admirable.
NORMAL / PATHOLOGICALDefined against a type at a stage of development, never in the abstract.
CATEGORY / ACTThe claim concerns crime as a feature of every society, not the moral status of any single crime or the fate of its victims.
INDIRECT / DIRECT USEIndirect: crime is the sign of collective feelings flexible enough to change. Direct: crime can anticipate a changed morality.
❓ THE STRONGEST OBJECTION

The pain analogy has an obvious function behind it: pain warns the body of damage. The function of any single crime is far harder to name. Durkheim’s reply is that his claim concerns the category, not the individual act. The objector can press further: a test built on how common a fact is cannot, by itself, condemn a society-wide evil, because the more widespread the evil, the more normal it becomes.

A second objection concerns Socrates. He is one famous case, chosen because history vindicated him. For every Socrates there may be many deviants history forgot, whose independence of thought was simply error. Durkheim’s argument needs deviance to be forward-looking on average, not every deviant to be admirable.

🎯 FOUR QUESTIONS
  1. What does Durkheim mean by normal? Is the sense statistical, functional, or normative?
  2. If a society eliminated its worst crimes, lesser faults would become crimes. Is this convincing? What historical evidence could count for or against it?
  3. Is Socrates a good example for Durkheim’s point? Does it support the argument?
  4. Does calling crime normal imply that criminals should not be punished? How do the claim that crime is normal and the claim that punishment serves a social function fit together?
✏️ AFTER THE QUESTIONS
▶  Question 3: is Socrates a fair example?
The example is strong because it does not soften the verdict. Durkheim does not say Athens was wrong by its own laws. He says the existence of people willing to be convicted for their convictions is part of how a society’s morality is able to move at all. The fair objection is selection: Socrates was vindicated, and most deviants are not. The argument holds if some deviance is forward-looking on average.
▶  Question 4: do the two halves fit together?
They fit because normal describes a fact’s place in the social order, not its moral standing or its treatment. A species-level fact about deviance and an act-level fact about one offender are different levels of description. Durkheim can hold that crime as a category is inevitable, even functional, and that a particular offender should be punished, because punishment does separate work: it reaffirms the collective conscience that the act violated, the boundary maintenance of Lesson 12.01.

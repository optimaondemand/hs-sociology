THE BOOK
Max Weber, "Class, Status, Party," Chapter VII of From Max Weber: Essays in Sociology, edited and translated by H. H. Gerth and C. Wright Mills. Any printing works. If your library holds a different edition or translation of Weber's writing on stratification, ask your facilitator before substituting it, since the wording below is specific to this translation.
START AT
The chapter's opening paragraph, on the relationship between economic power and social honor. It begins: “‘Economically conditioned’ power is not, of course, identical with ‘power’ as such.”
STOP AT
The end of the section defining parties, where Weber generalizes the concept beyond the modern world. It ends: “…despite the fact that their structure differs basically from the structure of modern parties.” If your printing continues from there into a further discussion of party organization and finance, that continuation is optional for this course.
HOW LONG
The assigned span runs roughly ten to fifteen pages depending on your printing, covering the definitions of class, status, and party in turn. Set aside about an hour.
WHAT TO SKIP
If you are short on time, skim Weber's illustrations of caste and ethnic segregation and his survey of how property becomes a status qualification. Those are supporting evidence. The three definitions and the closing pages on parties carry the argument you need for this week.
Page numbers are useless here because they differ between printings, which is why the boundaries above are given by section and by the words themselves. If the first words you find do not match, you are in the wrong place.

💡 WHERE THIS FITS
Wednesday's lesson gave you Weber's three dimensions as a finished framework, set against Marx's single axis. This essay is where Weber actually makes that case, sentence by sentence, against a position he takes seriously enough to grant most of its force before he starts qualifying it. Watch how much of Marx he concedes before he disagrees.
📖 WHY YOU ARE READING THIS

This is Weber's direct engagement with Marx's class analysis. He does not open by attacking it. He opens by separating power in general from economic power specifically, and only then builds toward the three-part typology. That order matters: Weber wants you to see that class, status, and party are all ways of distributing power before he shows you that they are not the same way.

The reading moves in four steps. Track them and the essay holds together.

1
Separate power from economic power. Power can be pursued for its own sake, or for the honor it brings, independent of wealth. This sets up everything that follows.
2
Define class as a market category. A class shares a common position in the labor or commodity market, nothing more. Watch for the phrase that ties class directly to "life chances."
3
Define status as social honor, and set it against class. Status groups are communities in a way classes are not, and status honor frequently runs against the pretensions of property rather than tracking it.
4
Define party as organized power, and generalize it beyond the modern world. Parties pursue power itself, for a cause or for their members, and Weber insists ancient and medieval groups can be parties too.
🎯 READ FOR THESE FOUR QUESTIONS
  1. What is the specific difference between class, as a market phenomenon, and status, as a social honor phenomenon, in Weber's account? Give an example he does not use of someone who is high class but low status, or low class but high status.
  2. Weber says the growth of status stratification causes "the hindrance of the free development of the market." What does he mean? How can social prestige and honor conflict with economic rationality?
  3. Weber introduces "party" as a third dimension of stratification distinct from both class and status. What is a party in his sense? Is it the same as a political party in ordinary usage, or broader?
  4. Marx would say that Weber's account of status and party ultimately reduces to class, that social honor and political power are reflections of economic position in the long run. Is this a convincing objection to Weber? What evidence would settle the dispute?
🔍 THREE SENTENCES TO FIND AND MARK

Three sentences carry the argument's structure. Find each one in your copy and mark it, then read the note underneath.

1. Class, defined. After separating power from wealth, Weber gives class a precise, narrow meaning:

“We may speak of a ‘class’ when a number of people have in common a specific causal component of their life chances, in so far as this component is represented exclusively by economic interests in the possession of goods and opportunities for income, and is represented under the conditions of the commodity or labor markets.”

"Class, Status, Party," opening section

Notice how much narrower this is than Marx's class. Weber's class is defined entirely by market position, your chances of getting goods and income through exchange, and by nothing about ownership of production as such. That narrowing is deliberate: it lets Weber ask whether market position alone explains everything about a person's social standing, which is precisely the question status and party are about to answer "no" to.

2. Status, set against class. Having defined class as a market category, Weber immediately insists status is not one:

“Status honor need not necessarily be linked with a ‘class situation.’ On the contrary, it normally stands in sharp opposition to the pretensions of sheer property.”

"Class, Status, Party," on status groups

"Sharp opposition" is a strong phrase, and Weber means it literally: old money frequently looks down on new money precisely because it is new, regardless of the size of the fortune. If status merely tracked class, this contempt would make no sense. Weber is using it as evidence that status has a logic of its own.

3. Party, as the third and separate house. Weber closes the typology by placing party in a domain neither class nor status occupies:

“But ‘parties’ live in a house of ‘power.’ Their action is oriented toward the acquisition of social ‘power,’ that is to say, toward influencing a communal action no matter what its content may be.”

"Class, Status, Party," on parties

"No matter what its content may be" is doing real work. A party, for Weber, is defined by its orientation toward organized power, not by what it wants the power for. That is why he can call ancient and medieval factions parties too, even though nothing about them resembles a modern political party's structure. Read on a few lines past this sentence and you will find him say exactly that.

✏️ AFTER YOU READ

Write short answers to the four questions in your Sociological Journal document. Question 1 will feed directly into tonight's journal entry, so answer that one with a real example.

▶  Answer question 2 in your notebook first, then open this.
Status groups protect certain goods, positions, or ways of life from being bought outright, which is exactly what a fully free market would allow. An old profession that refuses to let money alone determine entry, insisting instead on pedigree, apprenticeship, or manners, is withholding something from open exchange. Weber's point is that wherever status logic governs, price stops being the only thing that decides who gets what, and the market's normal solvent power, its ability to reduce every relationship to a transaction, is blocked. This is one of the clearest places in the essay where you can see status functioning as an independent force rather than as a shadow cast by wealth.
▶  Decide in your notebook whether Weber has actually refuted Marx, or only added detail to him. Then open this.
Notice that Weber never claims class is unimportant. He grants Marx the market analysis and then argues it is incomplete, not wrong. A committed Marxist could reply that this is exactly what you would expect: status and party look independent only if you stop the clock at a moment in history, and over the long run every status distinction and every party's power base traces back to who controls production. Weber has no knockdown answer to that reply within this essay; he is arguing that the three dimensions are useful for explaining particular cases, like the wealthy immigrant scenario from Wednesday's lesson, not that Marx's long-run claim about historical causation is false. Whether "useful for explaining cases" is a strong enough answer to "true in the long run" is exactly the disagreement you are being asked to take a position on, not one this guide will settle for you.

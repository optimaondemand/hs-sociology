📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Where Do You Fit?
Every known human society has been stratified, organized into layers or ranks that differ in their access to resources, prestige, and power. The sociological study of stratification asks how these hierarchies are organized, how they reproduce themselves across generations, how they shape individual experience, and whether and how they can be changed. Marx and Weber are the two towering figures in the sociology of stratification, and their differences are as instructive as their points of agreement.

📚 PART 1 · KEY TERMS
Class (Marx)
A group of people who share the same relationship to the means of production. Marx identifies two primary classes in capitalist society: the bourgeoisie, who own the means of production, and the proletariat, who own only their labor power. Class is an objective, economic category.
Class (Weber)
A group of people who share the same "life chances" in the market, the same probability of obtaining desired goods and achieving desired life outcomes through economic exchange. Weber's class is a market category, not a production category.
Status Group
Weber's term for a group defined by shared social honor or prestige rather than economic position. Status groups may or may not correspond to economic classes.
Party
Weber's term for a group organized around the acquisition of power. Parties are distinct from both classes (economic) and status groups (prestige), oriented instead toward influence and decision-making in a community.
Social Mobility
Movement between strata in a stratification system. Upward mobility means moving to a higher stratum, downward mobility means moving to a lower one. Open stratification systems permit relatively free mobility; closed systems, castes and estates, restrict it.
📚 PART 2 · MARX: CLASS AS THE ENGINE OF HISTORY

For Marx, class is the fundamental social division and the engine of historical change. Every society organized around private ownership of the means of production will divide into those who own, the ruling class, and those who do not, the exploited class. The specific form this division takes varies across epochs: masters and slaves in the ancient world, lords and serfs in feudalism, bourgeoisie and proletariat in capitalism, the argument you read last week in the Manifesto, Part I. But the structural logic is always the same. Those who control the means of production extract surplus from those who do not, and the cultural, legal, and political institutions of each epoch serve primarily to legitimize and maintain this extraction.

For Marx, class is not primarily about income or lifestyle but about structural position in the production process. A bourgeois who lost all her money would still be a member of the bourgeoisie if she owned productive property, and a proletarian who won the lottery would still be a proletarian if she then had to return to selling her labor. Class is about ownership, not wealth per se.

📚 PART 3 · WEBER: THREE DIMENSIONS OF STRATIFICATION

Weber accepts much of Marx's analysis but argues, in his essay "Class, Status, Party," that it is incomplete. Social stratification, he contends, has three distinct dimensions that do not always align: class, one's economic position in the market; status, one's social honor and prestige; and party, one's organized political power and influence. These dimensions can reinforce each other. A wealthy banker might have high status and political influence. But they can also diverge. A Catholic priest might have high status but no market-defined class. An ethnic minority might have wealth but low status.

THREE INDEPENDENT AXES
Weber, "Class, Status, Party." A person's position can be high on one and low on another.
DIMENSION 1
Class
Market situation. Your life chances in the economy: what you can obtain, and how reliably.
DIMENSION 2
Status
Social honor. How you are esteemed or scorned by others, independent of what you own.
DIMENSION 3
Party
Organized power. Your capacity to act with others toward a shared goal, a cause or a following.
Marx collapses all three into the first. Weber's claim is that the other two have lives of their own.

This multi-dimensional account has important practical implications. A purely economic class analysis would predict that a wealthy immigrant who is socially discriminated against would join forces with poor workers of the dominant ethnicity, since they share the same economic interests as non-owners. But in practice, this rarely happens: status group solidarity, ethnic, religious, racial, often overrides class solidarity. Weber's three-dimensional analysis can explain this. Marx's cannot easily.

📍 CASE IN POINT · WEBER’S AMERICAN EXAMPLE OF STATUS HONOR

To show that status is not the same thing as class, Weber reaches for the United States. Among American men who counted as gentlemen, he reports, the old tradition held that once the working day ended, the richest head of a firm was expected to treat his own clerk as an equal at cards or billiards in the club. Outside the subordination that business itself required, any other behaviour would have been thought repugnant. Property divided them; status did not.

He pairs this with a second American type, the political boss and the big speculator, who in his description hold real economic power while deliberately giving up social honor. The two cases carry the lesson’s distinction: a class situation is about goods and market chances, a status situation is about esteem, and the two can run in opposite directions in the same city. Weber gives the example without citing a source, so it records what he took to be common knowledge about American life in the early 1900s.

Source: Weber, Wirtschaft und Gesellschaft (1922), the section Klassen, Stände, Parteien (“Class, Status, Party”), written before 1914. Paraphrased.
📚 PART 4 · MAP FIVE PEOPLE ACROSS THREE DIMENSIONS

Weber's three questions: what is the market position, what is the social honor, and is there organized power?

Build a person the single-axis model cannot describe.

⚡ TRY IT · ALIGNED OR DIVERGENT?
Click each card. Predict whether this person's three dimensions line up or pull apart, then flip it.
CARD 1
A tenured professor, modest salary, widely respected, whose opinion pieces shape public debate.
ANALYSIS
Class: middling, salaried, not propertied. Status: high, honored as an expert. Party: real but indirect, through shaping opinion rather than organized following. Class and status diverge here, which income alone would miss.
CARD 2
A wealthy but widely distrusted moneylender, feared rather than respected, with no political organization behind him.
ANALYSIS
Class: high, strong market position. Status: low, wealth gained this way earns no honor and may cost it. Party: essentially none. A textbook case of class and status pulling in opposite directions.
CARD 3
A labor organizer, modest personal income, well liked in her neighborhood, elected to the city council.
ANALYSIS
Class: modest, similar to the workers she represents. Status: solidly respected locally. Party: high, organized political power that her income alone would never predict.
CARD 4
A recently arrived immigrant physician, high income, respected by colleagues, excluded from certain social clubs, without full political standing yet.
ANALYSIS
Class: high. Status: mixed, professional respect alongside social exclusion on other grounds. Party: low, no full political voice. This is close to Weber's own example of why one axis cannot capture what is happening to her.
CARD 5
A member of an old aristocratic family whose fortune was lost two generations ago.
ANALYSIS
Class: low by current market position. Status: still elevated, inherited honor can outlast the property that once justified it. Party: variable, sometimes retained through old networks. Status is lagging class here, a case Marx's single axis has trouble predicting.
📚 PART 5 · WHICH ACCOUNT IS RIGHT?

Neither, entirely. Marx captures the importance of ownership and structural position in shaping life chances; he is right that economic power is a major determinant of social position. But his reduction of all stratification to class obscures the independent influence of prestige and political power. Weber captures the multi-dimensionality of stratification and explains many phenomena that class analysis alone cannot, but his account is less powerful at explaining systematic, long-run patterns of exploitation and structural inequality. The most adequate account of stratification uses both frameworks: Marxian analysis to understand structural economic power, Weberian analysis to understand the full complexity of social position in all its dimensions.

▶  In your notebook, name a real case where class, status, and party diverge for someone you know of.
Test the case against Marx's likely reply: he would say that an apparent divergence is usually temporary or superficial, and that status and party eventually realign with underlying economic position given enough time. Weber would say some divergences are stable for generations, as with the aristocratic family in Card 5 above. The genuinely hard question is not whether divergence exists momentarily, everyone agrees it does, but whether it can persist indefinitely without economic position eventually reshaping it. That is close to the actual disagreement between a Marxian and a Weberian sociologist,.

💪 ONE AXIS OR THREE

Marx gives you one axis and a theory of history to go with it. Weber gives you three axes and no single story about which one wins in the end. Neither choice is free. Marx buys explanatory power for large-scale historical change at the cost of flattening cases like the ones you just sorted. Weber buys accuracy about individual cases at the cost of a tidy master narrative. Facing inequality honestly, this week's virtue, means resisting the temptation to pick whichever account makes the world simpler rather than whichever account fits the case in front of you.

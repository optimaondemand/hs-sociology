📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
When the way people work changes, what else changes with it?
On Monday you met a thinker who found a process nobody chose and nobody profits from. Today you meet one who says the outcome has beneficiaries and can be named. They are describing the same decades. Deciding which account explains more is your work, not theirs.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain why industrialisation matters sociologically for how it reorganised relationships rather than for how much it produced.
2
I will be able to assign a described experience to the right one of Marx's four separations, and defend the assignment.
3
I will be able to state where Marx's diagnosis and his political conclusions come apart, and why a sociologist can accept one without the other.
📚 PART 1 · THE WORLD TURNED UPSIDE DOWN

Between roughly 1760 and 1840 a series of technological and organisational innovations transformed the way human beings produced goods. The factory system replaced the cottage industry and the guild. The steam engine made available a source of power that dwarfed human and animal muscle. The railway connected markets that had been effectively isolated from one another. The result was a transformation of social life so rapid and so total that people who lived through it often felt they ended their lives in a different world from the one they were born into.

From a sociological standpoint the Industrial Revolution matters not primarily because it increased output, though it did so dramatically, but because it reorganised the social relationships within which production happens. That distinction is the whole reason this belongs in a sociology course rather than only in an economic history.

▶  In your notebook, list what changes for a weaver who moves from working at home to working in a factory. Then open this.
Most lists start with pay and hours, which are real and are the least interesting items on it. The sociologically loaded changes are these. She no longer owns her tools, so she cannot work without permission. She no longer sets her own pace, so the rhythm of her day is decided elsewhere. She no longer sells a finished cloth to a person, so she never meets whoever ends up with her work. She now works alongside people she did not choose, doing a fragment of a process whose whole she may never see. Notice that every one of those is a change in a relationship, not in a wage. That is the shift this lesson is about, and it is what Marx builds his account on.
📚 PART 2 · THE TERMS, AND THE ARGUMENT
TermWhat it means in Marx's usage
Mode of productionThe combination of the forces of production, meaning technology, raw materials, and human labour, with the relations of production, meaning the social arrangements within which production is organised and particularly who owns what. Each epoch of history has a distinctive one.
BourgeoisieThose who own the means of production: factories, raw materials, capital.
ProletariatThose who own only their labour power, which they must sell in order to live.
Surplus valueThe difference between the value a worker produces and the wage the worker is paid. Marx argues this difference is the source of profit, and that the wage relation makes the appropriation look like a fair exchange.
Alienation (Entfremdung)The condition of a worker separated from the product of her labour, from the process of her labour, from her fellow workers, and from her own nature as a creative being. Four separations, treated below.

Karl Marx was born in Trier, in what is now Germany, in 1818. He studied philosophy in Berlin, was radicalised by his encounter with Hegelian philosophy and with the conditions of early industrial capitalism, and spent most of his adult life in exile in London, writing and organising. The Communist Manifesto (1848), written with Friedrich Engels, and the first volume of Capital (1867) are among the most influential texts in modern history, and you read part of the first of them tomorrow.

His central argument is that capitalism is a historically specific mode of production, characterised by private ownership of the means of production and by the exploitation of wage labour. The capitalist owns the factory. The worker owns only her labour power and sells it for a wage. Marx argues that the wage is systematically less than the value she produces, and that the difference is taken as profit.

The part worth slowing down for is his claim about how the arrangement holds. Feudalism was maintained by force, and everybody could see the force. Capitalism, on his account, is maintained by the wage relation itself, which presents as a free contract between equals while functioning as structural coercion, because the worker must sell her labour or go without. Whether you find that persuasive, notice what kind of claim it is. He is not saying anyone is lying. He is saying a relationship can be voluntary in form and compelled in substance.

📍 CASE IN POINT · FROM THE MEDIEVAL BURGHERS TO THE BOURGEOISIE

Marx and Engels treat the bourgeoisie as a people with a history, and they sketch it in a paragraph. Earlier societies had many ranks: “In ancient Rome we have patricians, knights, plebeians, slaves; in the Middle Ages, feudal lords, vassals, guild-masters, journeymen, apprentices, serfs.” The new class grew out of the old towns: “From the serfs of the Middle Ages sprang the chartered burghers of the earliest towns. From these burgesses the first elements of the bourgeoisie were developed.” Its political form varied by country: in their words, an independent urban republic in Italy and Germany, a taxable third estate of the monarchy in France.

The argument needs this history: if class is the engine, the ruling class of 1848 must itself have been produced by earlier struggles. Note the form of the evidence. The Manifesto is a political pamphlet, and its history carries no dates and cites no sources. Which of its claims could a historian check, and against what?

Source: Marx and Engels, Manifesto of the Communist Party, ch. I (1848), trans. Samuel Moore (1888, public domain).
📚 PART 3 · FOUR SEPARATIONS
ALIENATION, IN FOUR PARTS
Marx is describing separations, not moods. Each one is a link that used to exist and no longer does.
SEPARATION 1
From the product
She does not own what she makes and has no relationship to it once it leaves the building. It goes to someone she will never meet.
SEPARATION 2
From the process
She does not decide how the work is done, only whether she does it. The pace, the sequence, and the method are set elsewhere.
SEPARATION 3
From other workers
The labour market places her in competition with the people beside her for the job she holds, so the people nearest her are structurally her rivals.
SEPARATION 4
From her own nature
Marx holds that human beings are creative, self-directing, productive creatures by nature. Work that expresses none of that separates a person from what she is.
THE CRAFTSMAN MARX CONTRASTS
Owned his tools. Set his own hours. Made a whole object. Sold it to a person he knew. The work carried his name.
THE INDUSTRIAL WORKER
Owns nothing. Keeps hours set by others. Makes a fragment. Sells time rather than goods. The work is anonymous.
⚠️ A FAIR OBJECTION
The craftsman on the left is doing a lot of work in this argument, and historians have questioned him. Guild craftsmen were often poor, constrained by guild rules, and not obviously freer than a factory worker with a wage. If the contrast is romanticised, the sense of loss may be too. Marx's defenders reply that the comparison is structural rather than nostalgic: the point is not that the craftsman was happier but that his relationship to his work had features the wage relation removes. Decide for yourself whether that reply holds.
⚡ WHICH SEPARATION?
Each description below is one of the four separations, or none of them. Judge by which link has been broken, not by how unpleasant the situation sounds.
1. A skilled worker spends her shift on one repeated motion that uses almost nothing she is capable of.
2. A worker assembles components that ship to a customer she will never meet, and owns none of them.
3. Several workers know that only some of them will keep their posts after the review, and all of them know it.
4. A self-employed carpenter works punishing hours to finish a commission she chose, using her own tools, for a client she knows.
5. A worker follows a sequence and a pace fixed by a system she had no part in designing and cannot change.
📚 PART 4 · WEBER AND MARX ON THE SAME DECADES

Put Monday's lesson beside today's. Both thinkers are looking at industrial Europe and both conclude that something has gone wrong with modern work. They disagree about what kind of thing it is.

WeberMarx
What went wrongRationalisation. Efficiency became an end, and other values found no purchase in the institutions.Exploitation and alienation. Ownership of the means of production separates a worker from her work.
Who is responsibleNobody in particular. Every step was a reasonable decision to do something better.A class with an interest in the arrangement continuing.
Where the problem livesIn the form of organisation itself.In who owns what.
What would fix itHe does not say. He appears not to have known.Changing the ownership of the means of production.

The last row is the sharpest difference and the most useful one for you. A diagnosis that names a beneficiary implies an action. A diagnosis that names a process may not. Which of those is a strength depends on whether the beneficiary is really there, and that is an empirical question rather than a matter of taste.

▶  Write down whether you can accept Marx's account of alienation without accepting his politics. Then open this.
Many sociologists do exactly that, and it is worth understanding why it is coherent rather than evasive. Alienation is a claim about what a particular arrangement of work does to the people inside it, and it can be investigated: you can ask workers, compare arrangements, and look for the separations. Whether the remedy is to change who owns the factories is a further claim, and it does not follow automatically, because a separation might be reduced by other means, or might turn out to persist under different ownership. Holding the diagnosis while contesting the prescription is ordinary practice in social science. It is the same move available with Weber, whose account of the iron cage many accept without accepting his pessimism about remedies. Whichever way you come down, the discipline asks you to say which part you are accepting and on what evidence.

💪 WHAT YOU NOW KNOW

Marx's diagnosis of alienation is powerful, and its force does not depend on accepting all of his political conclusions. Whether or not the remedy he proposed is the right one, the alienation itself is a real phenomenon that sociology has to take seriously, and every thinker you meet in the remaining weeks is working somewhere in the space he and Weber opened between them. Your journal this week asks you to hold both accounts at once on a single case. That is harder than choosing, and it is the point.

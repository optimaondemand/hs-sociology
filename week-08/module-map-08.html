Week 8: Nisbet and Putnam on Institutions and Social Capital
Start here. This page shows you everything the week contains.
❓ THE QUESTION THIS WEEK IS ABOUT
If Tocqueville was right in 1840, would we be able to tell by now?
This week closes a thread that has been running since Week 3. Tocqueville warned that democratic equality could dissolve the associations that hold a free society together. Nisbet, in 1953, argued that it already had, and that the result was a crisis of belonging that fed the century's totalitarian movements. Putnam, writing in the 1990s, went looking for evidence that the decline was real and measurable. One argument, three thinkers, a century and a half apart.
A NOTE ON THIS WEEK'S TWO READINGS
Both readings this week come from Tocqueville, Democracy in America, Volume II, Part II, and they sit two chapters apart in his own text. They are not the same passage. Read the difference before you start.
Tuesday · Guide 8A
Chapters VI–VII. Political associations: forming a party, running a campaign, and the free press that connects them. Pairs with Monday's lesson on Nisbet.
Thursday · Guide 8B
Chapter V, the chapter before it. Civil associations: everything Americans organize for that is not political, from temperance societies to hospitals. This is the assigned reading for Wednesday's Putnam lesson, in place of Putnam's own book. See below.

💡 THE VIRTUE THIS WEEK IS SERVICE
Service here means the ordinary, unglamorous habit of acting for a common good that is not your own: joining the committee, showing up to the meeting, taking the shift nobody wants. Tocqueville, Nisbet, and Putnam all treat that habit as more than a nicety. It is the thing a free society runs on. The wrap-up survey will ask you to name one intermediate institution you actually belong to, and what you do inside it.

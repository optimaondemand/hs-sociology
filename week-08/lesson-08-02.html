📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Could you prove that a nation is joining fewer things together, and that it matters?
Robert Putnam set out to do exactly that. His answer runs through a title most people remember and an argument most people do not, and the argument is not new. He is measuring something Tocqueville warned about in 1840.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to define social capital and distinguish its bonding form from its bridging form using cases the definitions do not obviously settle.
2

📚 PART 1 · BOWLING ALONE
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Between 1980 and 1993, the number of Americans who bowled rose by about 10 percent, but membership in organized bowling leagues fell by about 40 percent. More people bowling, fewer bowling together. Guess: what do you think explains that gap? (No wrong answer.)

In 1995, Robert Putnam published an article with one of the most striking titles in contemporary social science: "Bowling Alone: America's Declining Social Capital." The title referred to the specific, measurable phenomenon above, and Putnam used it as his emblem for a broader pattern he documented across dozens of indicators: Americans were participating less in civic organizations, voting less frequently, trusting their neighbors and institutions less, and joining fewer of the voluntary associations that Tocqueville had identified as the lifeblood of American democracy.

Putnam's diagnosis was not merely nostalgic. He argued that the decline of what he called social capital, the networks of trust, reciprocity, and civic engagement that make cooperative social life possible, had measurable consequences for democratic governance, economic performance, public health, and individual well-being. Communities with high social capital had better schools, lower crime rates, healthier residents, and more effective governments. Social capital was not merely pleasant. It was productive in the deepest sense.

Key Terms
Social Capital
The networks of relationships, trust, and reciprocity that enable people to act collectively and cooperate for mutual benefit. Like physical or financial capital, it is a resource that can be accumulated, invested, and depleted.
Bonding Social Capital
Social capital that connects similar people: members of the same ethnic group, neighborhood, religious community, or social class. It creates strong internal bonds but can also create exclusivity.
Bridging Social Capital
Social capital that connects different people across social divides: across class, ethnicity, religion, and neighborhood. It is weaker per tie than bonding capital but broader in reach, and Putnam argues it is more important for democratic health.
Civic Engagement
Participation in the public life of a community: voting, joining voluntary associations, attending public meetings, volunteering. Putnam treats it as both an expression of social capital and a means of generating more.
📚 PART 2 · THE EVIDENCE FOR DECLINE

Putnam marshals evidence across a wide range of indicators. Membership in civic organizations, the PTA, the League of Women Voters, fraternal organizations, labor unions, declined sharply from the 1960s through the 1990s. Voter turnout fell. Participation in informal social activities, dinner parties, club meetings, neighborhood organizations, declined. Survey measures of trust in other people and in institutions fell substantially. Putnam identified television, and later electronic entertainment more broadly, as one significant cause: time spent in passive, private entertainment displaces time available for civic engagement. Suburban sprawl was another: longer commutes and auto-dependent development reduce the daily occasions for informal social interaction.

Notice what kind of claim this is. Putnam is not arguing from impression or nostalgia. He is doing what Durkheim did with suicide records in Week 3: assembling decades of institutional membership rolls, voter files, and survey data and asking whether the pattern is real, how large it is, and what tracks with it. A critic can dispute his causes without disputing his numbers, and the strongest objections to Putnam usually take that form.

📍 CASE IN POINT · THE AMERICAN TEMPERANCE SOCIETIES OF THE 1830s

Before there was a decline to measure, Tocqueville recorded the baseline. “The first time I heard in the United States that 100,000 men had bound themselves publicly to abstain from spirituous liquors, it appeared to me more like a joke than a serious engagement.” He came to read it differently: “300,000 Americans, alarmed by the progress of drunkenness around them, had made up their minds to patronize temperance.” The contrast he draws is with his own country: “if these 100,000 men had lived in France, each of them would singly have memorialized the government to watch the public-houses all over the kingdom.”

This is the kind of association Putnam later counted. Tocqueville gives the figures without saying where he got them, so treat them as his report rather than a census. The comparison is the analytic part: the same public concern, met in one country by petitioning the state and in the other by forming a society.

Source: Tocqueville, Democracy in America II.2.5 (1840), trans. Henry Reeve (public domain).
📚 PART 3 · BONDING OR BRIDGING?

The two forms of social capital are not always easy to tell apart in practice, and Putnam thinks the mix matters more than the total amount. A society thick with bonding capital and thin on bridging capital can still be deeply divided along the lines that bonding reinforces. Place each association on the scale from pure bonding to pure bridging.

📚 PART 4 · ONE DIAGNOSIS, THREE MOMENTS

At the end of the quarter, it is worth seeing plainly how this argument was assembled, because it was not invented in 1995. Tocqueville stated the danger in 1840, without any way to measure it at national scale. Nisbet, in 1953, explained the mechanism by which the danger becomes a crisis. Putnam, writing in the 1990s, is the one who finally had the membership rolls, voting records, and survey archives to ask whether Tocqueville's fear had come true, and he opens his own book by saying so directly.

ONE DIAGNOSIS, THREE MOMENTS
Putnam is not proposing a new theory. He is testing an old one against a century and a half of data.
1840
Tocqueville
Warns
Democratic equality dissolves the old vertical bonds of lord and guild, and a nation without something to replace them risks a mild but total government that governs a population of strangers.
→
1953
Nisbet
Explains the mechanism
Names the missing layer precisely: family, church, guild, neighborhood. Their loss produces the alienation that fed the century's mass movements.
→
1995
Putnam
Measures it
Uses membership rolls, voting records, and surveys, data neither predecessor had, to show a real, dated decline in American associational life.
Three different kinds of evidence for one claim: a warning, a mechanism, and a measurement.

⚡ MEET THE EVIDENCE · TAP EACH CARD
Each front names one of Putnam's findings. Tap to see what it was measuring and why he considered it significant.
Bowling league membership fell about 40 percent from 1980 to 1993, while individual bowling rose
The image behind his title. Participation in the activity did not disappear. Its organized, associational form did, which is exactly the distinction between activity and association that his whole argument depends on.
Sharp membership declines in the PTA, the League of Women Voters, fraternal orders, and labor unions from the 1960s onward
These are exactly the intermediate institutions Nisbet worried about a generation earlier. Their decline is Nisbet's diagnosis, now dated and quantified.
Falling survey measures of trust in neighbors and public institutions across the same decades
Putnam treats trust as both a product of associational life and a resource that keeps it running. When it falls alongside membership, the two declines can reinforce each other.
📚 PART 5 · IS THE EXPLANATION BIG ENOUGH?

Putnam's numbers are widely accepted. His causes are not. Television and suburban sprawl are real and measurable, but critics have asked whether they are large enough to explain a decline of the size Putnam documents, across nearly every category of association at once. A generational account, that a specific cohort simply joined less and never taught the habit to the next one, explains some of the timing better than a single external cause like television can. Putnam takes generational replacement seriously in his fuller work, but the honest state of the argument is that no single cause fully accounts for the scale of the change, and that is worth knowing before you treat the diagnosis as closed.

▶  In your notebook, name one thing that has changed in civic or associational life since 2000 that Putnam could not have seen coming. Then open this.
Two candidates are worth weighing against each other. Social media plainly did not exist in the form Putnam studied, and it complicates his diagnosis in both directions: it has generated new bridging ties across distance while research since has found it a poor substitute for the local, embodied associations his data actually measured, since a shared feed is not the same as a shared meeting. Separately, some forms of civic participation he judged in decline, mutual aid networks and informal community organizing among them, have grown in visibility since 2000 in ways his 1990s indicators were not built to capture. Neither development refutes Putnam outright. Both suggest that "decline" may describe a change in form as much as a change in amount, and a good sociologist updates the measurement before declaring the trend reversed or confirmed.

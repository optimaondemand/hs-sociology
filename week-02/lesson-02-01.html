📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What would your own life look like to a stranger who had never seen it?
Last week you learned that sociology began when familiar arrangements came apart. Today you learn the technique that lets you study arrangements that have not come apart: looking at your own culture as though you had just arrived in it.

2

3

📚 PART 1 · THE NACIREMA
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Predict before you read: a researcher describes a tribe whose members keep a shrine room in every house and insert bundles of hog hair into their mouths twice a day. How far away do you think this tribe lives? (No wrong answer.)

In 1956, the anthropologist Horace Miner published a paper in American Anthropologist (58:3) describing the ritual practices of a North American tribe called the Nacirema. The Nacirema, Miner reported, are obsessed with the body, which they consider fundamentally ugly and prone to decay. Each household maintains a shrine room devoted to bodily rituals. Twice daily, family members insert small hog-hair bundles into their mouths and perform highly ritualized movements while applying magical powders. They visit medicine men in grand ceremonial temples filled with vials of liquid. Specialists torture their patients to drive out the evil spirits inhabiting their teeth. Children are inducted into these rituals at an early age through formal instruction by elders.

By the third paragraph, most readers work out that Nacirema is American spelled backward. The shrine room is a bathroom. The hog-hair bundles are toothbrushes. The ceremonial temple is a hospital. The specialists are dentists.

Miner's point is not to mock American culture. It is to show how familiar practices look strange when described with the detachment an anthropologist would use on a culture not their own. This is one of sociology's core techniques. What you have always taken for granted becomes an object you can actually examine, and you cannot examine what you cannot first see.

📚 PART 2 · THE FIVE COMPONENTS OF CULTURE

Culture is not a single thing but a system of interrelated parts. Five terms carry the rest of this course.

ComponentWhat it covers
CultureThe whole of a group's shared knowledge, beliefs, values, norms, language, symbols, and material objects. Learned, never inherited genetically, and passed on through socialization.
NormsFrom the Latin norma, a carpenter's square or rule. The shared expectations that govern behavior. They come in four grades of seriousness, below.
ValuesThe general principles a culture holds as desirable. More abstract than norms. Values are the goods that norms protect.
SymbolAny object, word, gesture, or image carrying a meaning assigned by those who share the culture. Language is the most important symbol system, but flags, uniforms, and colors count too.
Material cultureThe physical objects and technology a culture produces: tools, buildings, clothing, food, art. It both reflects and reinforces everything non-material.

Knowledge is the body of beliefs a culture treats as true: how crops grow, where the sun goes at night, what causes illness. Not all of it is accurate by scientific standards, but all of it is real in the sense that it shapes behavior. Beliefs shading toward the sacred are what anthropologists call religion; beliefs about how society should be organized are ideology.

Language is the component all the others depend on. Without it, culture cannot pass across generations and the accumulation of knowledge that separates human civilization from animal sociality cannot happen. The linguist Edward Sapir argued, in “The Status of Linguistics as a Science” (1929), that language does not merely describe a reality that was already there; it partly constitutes the reality of the speaker. A culture with one word for several distinct experiences has, in a meaningful sense, fewer distinctions available to it than a culture that separates them. Whether that view is entirely right is still debated. What is not debated is that language is the main vehicle by which culture is learned.

Norms are the behavioral specifications of a culture's values. A culture that values honesty develops norms against lying. A culture that values modesty develops norms about dress. A culture that values education develops norms about how students and teachers behave. Look at what a culture forbids most fiercely and you can read backward to what it holds most dear.

📚 PART 3 · FOUR GRADES OF NORM
THE LADDER OF NORMS
Same idea, four degrees of seriousness. What happens when you break it tells you which rung you are on.
Folkways
mild disapproval
Minor conventions. Breaking one makes you rude or odd, not wicked.
Eating with your mouth open. Interrupting a speaker. Standing too close in a queue.
Mores
moral censure
Rules held with real moral weight. Breaking one makes you a bad person in others' eyes.
Dishonesty. Cruelty. Infidelity. Abandoning someone who depends on you.
Laws
formal penalty
Norms a political authority has written down, with a stated punishment attached.
Speed limits. Theft. Contract obligations. Tax filing.
Taboos
near unthinkable
The most severely held prohibitions, often tied to the sacred or the dangerous. Violation registers as horror rather than disapproval.
Eating human flesh. Desecrating the dead.

💡 WORTH NOTICING
The same behavior can sit on a different rung in a different culture, or in the same culture at a different time. That is not a flaw in the categories. It is the most useful thing about them. When a behavior moves from one rung to another, something has changed in what the culture values, and the movement is easier to see than the value.
⚡ TRY IT · WHICH KIND OF NORM?
Sort each rule by what would actually happen to someone who broke it in the contemporary United States. Judge by the consequence, not by how much you personally disapprove.
1. Chewing with your mouth open at a family dinner.
2. Driving twenty miles per hour over the posted limit.
3. Being unfaithful to a spouse.
4. Eating human flesh.
5. Cutting into a conversation before the other person finishes.
6. Lying to a friend to avoid an awkward conversation.
📚 PART 4 · CULTURE IS NOT SOCIETY

Culture and society are related but they are not synonyms, and using them interchangeably will cost you precision for the rest of this course.

A society is a group of people who share a territory and a set of interdependent relationships. A culture is the system of meaning, practice, and belief those people share. The same territory can hold many cultures at once, in the form of subcultures, countercultures, and immigrant communities. The same cultural tradition can persist across quite different political societies.

Two examples make the distinction concrete. Medieval Europe was many political societies but, in important respects, a single Latin Christian culture. Modern America is one political society containing dozens of distinct subcultures with their own norms, vocabularies, and practices. To study a society sociologically you must study its culture. That does not make them the same object.

▶  In your notebook, name one subculture you belong to and two of its norms that would puzzle an outsider. Then open this.
The test of whether you have found a real subculture norm rather than just a preference is this: could you break it, and would other members notice and mind? A shared taste is not a norm. A rule about how you speak, dress, greet, or rank one another inside the group is. If both of your examples turned out to be tastes, try again with the question of what would get you gently corrected by someone senior in the group. That correction is the norm doing its work.

📚 PART 1 · THE BOY FOUND IN THE FOREST

In January 1800, a boy of roughly eleven or twelve was found living alone in the forests of southern France, near the town of Saint-Sernin-sur-Rance. He had no language. He could not hold sustained eye contact. He could not play, could not be affectionate, and did not respond to human speech. He ate acorns and raw potatoes. He showed no modesty and no sense of shame, and he did not react to cold the way a boy of his apparent age would. The villagers who found him named him Victor. A physician named Jean Marc Gaspard Itard undertook to educate him, and published reports on the attempt in 1801 and 1806.

Itard failed in the narrow sense. Victor never learned more than a few words, and never acquired the range of behavior his contemporaries considered the marks of civilization. But the case showed starkly something that had been theorized and never so plainly illustrated: the human capacity for social life is not simply issued by nature. It has to be developed through sustained interaction with other people, and at certain points in development rather than at any point.

Take that process away and what goes missing is not manners. It is the basic architecture of a social self. Victor had not been socialized, and the absence of socialization had left him, in the social sense of the word, not yet a person among persons.

▶  Before reading on, write in your notebook: what does Victor's case prove, and what does it fail to prove? Then open this.
It shows that some capacities we assume are automatic do not develop without social contact at the right time. That is a real and important finding. What it cannot show is how much of Victor's condition came from isolation and how much from something else. Nobody knows why he was in the forest, how long he was there, or whether he had a disability before he was abandoned. A single case with unknown starting conditions cannot separate those causes, and honest sociology says so. Itard's records are careful, and they are still one boy. Hold the finding and the limit together. That habit is more valuable than either one alone.
📚 PART 2 · HOW A PERSON GETS A CULTURE
THE AGENTS OF SOCIALIZATION
Each one teaches a different lesson, in a different way, at a different stage.
INFANCY ONWARD
Family
Language, the first norms, and the basic sense that the world is predictable or is not. Organised by love and by particular relationships.
EARLY CHILDHOOD ONWARD
School
Authority that is not parental, impersonal rules, evaluation by a standard applied to everyone. How to wait, and how to take instruction from strangers.
CHILDHOOD AND ADOLESCENCE
Peer group
Horizontal and informal. Status, loyalty, belonging, and identity worked out among equals rather than handed down.
THROUGHOUT LIFE
Media
Models of how people speak, want, argue, and live, supplied at scale by strangers you will never meet.
PRIMARY SOCIALIZATION
SECONDARY SOCIALIZATION
Primary socialization builds the self. Secondary socialization teaches that self how to operate inside particular institutions.

Socialization is the lifelong process by which people learn and internalize the norms, values, language, and roles of their culture. It is how culture moves from one generation to the next, and how an individual becomes a functioning member of a society.

Primary socialization is the first and most foundational phase, happening in early childhood inside the family. It covers the acquisition of language, the basic norms of behavior, and the fundamental sense of self. Secondary socialization is everything after: learning the norms and roles specific to particular institutions once the basic self is in place. An agent of socialization is any person, group, or institution that significantly shapes what someone values, believes, and takes themselves to be.

One more term, and it is the one worth remembering longest. In Human Nature and the Social Order (1902), Charles Horton Cooley described what he called the looking-glass self: we build our self-concept by imagining how we appear to other people and then taking that imagined judgment inside. On this account the self is not a private thing that later goes out to meet society. It is social all the way down.

📚 PART 3 · THE FAMILY AS THE FIRST SOCIETY

The family is the primary agent of socialization for one simple reason: it is where most human beings first meet other human beings. Before school, before peers, before any formal institution, the infant meets a particular set of people who give or withhold warmth, speak a particular language, keep particular habits of eating and sleeping and greeting, and respond to behavior in particular ways.

This is not mainly a matter of learning rules. It is the formation of the basic grammar of social life. Through repeated interaction the infant learns that other people have intentions, that their own behavior has effects on others, that certain responses can be anticipated, and that the world is either broadly predictable and trustworthy or is not.

Language is the central achievement of primary socialization and its main vehicle. Acquiring a language is not just acquiring labels for objects that were already there. It is acquiring the conceptual structure through which one particular culture organizes reality. To learn English first is to learn to divide time into past, present, and future in a way that is not universal across languages. To learn a language with elaborate grammatical markers of social status is to be trained to notice and answer to status in every sentence you speak.

School and peer group

When a child enters school she meets a social world running on different rules. The family is organised by love and by particular relationships. The school is organised by authority, achievement, and impersonal rules. She must learn to answer to adults who are not her parents, to compete and cooperate with peers she did not choose, and to submit to evaluation by a standard applied to every student alike. As the education researcher Philip Jackson observed in Life in Classrooms (1968), these lessons are often more consequential for adult life than the academic content: how to wait, how to follow instructions from strangers, and how to manage yourself in a crowd.

Peer groups do something different again. Family socialization flows downward from adults to children, and school socialization flows from authority figures to students, but peer socialization runs sideways, among equals. Peer groups build their own norms, status hierarchies, vocabulary, and practices. They are the main setting in which adolescents work out questions of identity, loyalty, and belonging, which is part of why they matter so much and feel so consuming at the time.

📚 PART 4 · NATURE, CULTURE, AND AN ARGUMENT THAT IS NOT OVER

Victor's story raises a question sociology cannot fully answer: how much of human social behavior is shaped by biology and how much by socialization? This is the nature and nurture controversy, and it has not been resolved. It probably cannot be resolved cleanly, because it is impossible to raise a human being outside of any social environment, and nobody should try.

What sociology contributes is evidence that even capacities which look obviously natural, including emotional expression, cognitive development, and moral reasoning, depend on social experience in order to develop at all. The useful move is not to pick a side but to separate the capacity from its content. The capacity for language is biological; the particular language you speak is entirely cultural. The capacity for attachment is biological; whom you attach to, and how you show it, are shaped by socialization.

That distinction is worth practising, because it is the difference between a sociological claim and a slogan.

⚡ CAPACITY OR CONTENT?
Place each item on the spectrum. The question is not whether biology is involved at all, since it always is. The question is whether this particular thing is fixed by biology or filled in by the culture you grew up in.
Which language you speak fluently
The ability to acquire a language at all
What counts as polite behavior at a shared meal
Feeling grief when someone close to you dies
How openly you are expected to show grief in public

The conclusion is not a small one. What human beings are, in their fully developed form, is always and irreducibly a joint product of nature and culture. Neither term alone will finish the sentence.

💪 WHAT YOU NOW KNOW

You now have an answer to the question this week opened with. A great deal of what feels most like you was handed to you, by a family you did not choose, in a language you did not select, through a process you cannot remember. That is not a bleak conclusion. Cooley's point is that there was never a private self waiting underneath to be freed. The self was built in company. What you can now do is notice the building, which is more than most people ever do.
